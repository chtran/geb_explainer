Three-Part Invention + The MU-puzzle

GEB

A race that, by one argument, can never be won, and a puzzle that looks easy. Followed one step at a time, from the inside, both can go on forever. Step outside and look at the whole thing, and both are settled in a few lines. This page covers the dialogue Three-Part Invention and Chapter I, where Hofstadter introduces formal systems with a game of just three letters.

Dialogue
Three-Part Invention
1

Achilles, the Tortoise, and the man who invented them

The story. Achilles (the Greek hero, fastest runner alive) and the Tortoise stand on a dusty running track in the hot sun. At the far end flies a red flag with a thin ring cut out of it: a zero, the Tortoise says, the favourite number of the flag's owner, Zeno. Achilles points out that zero hasn't been invented yet, so the flag can't exist. The Tortoise agrees, and admires it anyway.

Achilles tries to tell a Zen koan (a short Buddhist puzzle story meant to jolt the mind out of ordinary reasoning). Two monks argue about a flag: one says the flag is moving, the other says the wind is. A wise master tells them: neither, the mind is moving. Achilles garbles it, crediting the master's line to "Zeno". Then a breeze comes up and the two of them act it out: "the flag is moving!" "No, the wind is!"

Zeno himself walks up and declares that nothing is moving: motion is impossible, which he proudly calls his Theorem. To prove it he tells his most famous story. Achilles races a tortoise that has a head start, and can never catch it. Zeno then sets up a real race to the flag, gives the Tortoise a head start of ten rods (about 50 metres), and calls "On your mark! Get set! Go!" The dialogue ends there. We never learn who wins.

The form: a three-part invention

Bach wrote 15 inventions for two voices and 15 for three (he called the three-voice ones Sinfonias), finished in 1723 as lessons for keyboard students. Each is built from one small musical idea, passed from voice to voice. His title page says they teach students to play cleanly in several parts, to come up with good ideas and develop them, and to get a first taste of composing.

This dialogue has three voices too: Achilles, the Tortoise and Zeno, who pick up and toss around one idea, motion. Below is a short sketch of the form: one idea, entering in a high, middle and low voice in turn. (An original sketch in Bach's style, not his notes.)

The hidden jokeAn invention is also something someone made up. Achilles and the Tortoise first appear in one of Zeno's paradoxes (puzzles that seem to prove something impossible), around 450 BC, so Zeno really did invent them. In the dialogue he calls them two characters of his own Invention. Hofstadter borrowed them from Lewis Carroll, who had borrowed them from Zeno. Here, the inventor walks into his own story and meets his creations.

Two more jokes set up the chapter. Zeno's grand "Theorem" with a capital T is exactly the kind of word Chapter I takes apart: in a formal system, a theorem will mean something much humbler. And the koan's answer refuses both options it was offered ("neither the flag nor the wind"). Chapter I has a name for that kind of answer, too.

2

Zeno's race: inside the argument, and outside it

Zeno of Elea (born around 490 BC) argued that motion is an illusion. His Achilles story goes like this. To pass the Tortoise, Achilles must first reach the spot where the Tortoise was. By then the Tortoise has crawled a little further. So Achilles must reach that spot, and by then the Tortoise has moved on again… There's always one more step, so (says Zeno) Achilles never catches up.

Let's run it. Simplified numbers: the Tortoise starts 50 metres ahead, and Achilles runs twice as fast (10 metres a second against an unusually sporty Tortoise's 5).

Zeno steps: 0Gap left: 50 mTime used: 0 s
Each Zeno step, Achilles runs to where the Tortoise was. Click a few times.
Time, split into Zeno's steps

Inside the argument, you only ever ask "where is the Tortoise now?" and take one more step. The steps never run out, so the argument never reaches an end. Outside it, you look at all the steps at once and add them up: 5 + 2.5 + 1.25 + 0.625 + … seconds. The pieces halve each time, and together they fill exactly 10 seconds, like cutting a bar in half, then the leftover in half, forever, without ever making the bar longer. After 10 seconds Achilles draws level, and then he's ahead. (Mathematicians made this precise in the 1800s. Philosophers still debate whether that fully answers Zeno.)

The idea the dialogue sets upA process can be endless when followed one step at a time, and still easy to settle when you step back and look at the whole. The chapter now explains this difference between working inside a system, obeying its rules move by move, and stepping outside to reason about it. It does so with a puzzle you can try yourself.
Chapter I
The MU-puzzle
3

A game played with symbols

The Introduction described a formal system: starting statements plus mechanical rules for making new ones, like a board game's starting position and its legal moves. Chapter I builds a tiny example, so you can see what "following the rules and nothing else" really feels like.

Words for this chapter. A string is just a row of symbols, like MIUU. The axiom is the string you're given to start with. A rule of production is a rule that lets you make a new string from one you already have. A theorem is any string you can make from the axiom using the rules. The record of how you made it, one string per line, is its derivation.

Notice that here "theorem" doesn't mean "an important truth". Zeno's grand Theorem was a claim about the world. A theorem of a formal system is only a string the rules let you write down. It doesn't mean anything at all.

The MIU-system

The only symbols are the three letters M, I and U. The axiom is MI. There are four rules. In them, x stands for any string at all, even an empty one.

Rule I
xI → xIU

If a string ends in I, you may add a U at the end.

Example: MI → MIU
Rule II
Mx → Mxx

You may double everything after the M.

Example: MIU → MIUIU
Rule III
III → U

Anywhere you see III, you may replace those three letters with one U.

Example: MUIIIU → MUUU
Rule IV
UU → (nothing)

Anywhere you see UU, you may delete it.

Example: MUUU → MU

Rules work in one direction only: rule III turns III into U, but no rule turns U back into III. And you may only do what the rules allow. No inventing shortcuts, no "obviously this works". Hofstadter calls this the requirement of formality.

The rule examples above end in MU, but that chain didn't start from the axiom MI. Here is the puzzle: starting from MI, can you make MU?

    5

    Working inside the system, and stepping outside it

    If you tried for MU, something probably happened after a while. You stopped just applying rules and started thinking about them: "doubling gives me more I's, and rule III removes them three at a time… what am I actually able to reach?" Hofstadter names the two attitudes:

    M-mode

    Mechanical

    Work inside the system. Apply rules, look at the result, apply more. Never get bored, never ask why. A simple computer program that just applies rules works like this, and can grind on forever without noticing anything.

    I-mode

    Intelligent

    Step outside. Notice patterns in what you've made, guess what's always true, decide a path is hopeless. Hofstadter sees this ability to jump out of the task you're doing and survey it as a mark of intelligence.

    U-mode

    Un-mode

    The Zen way: refuse the question's own terms, as the koan in the dialogue refused both "the flag" and "the wind". Hofstadter only names it here and returns to it in later chapters.

    The names spell the system's letters: M, I, U. Here's some I-mode practice: is each statement always true? To show one is false, a single example is enough. To show one is always true, you can't check every string, because there are infinitely many. You have to reason about the rules instead.

    "Every theorem starts with M."

    "Applying a rule never makes a string shorter."

    "Every theorem has exactly one M."

    The "always true" answers came from looking at the rules from outside, not from making more strings. None of these facts can even be written as a string of the MIU-system: the system only makes strings, it can't say anything about them. That's the gap between working in a system and reasoning about it, and it's a theme Gödel's proof will exploit later in the book.

    6

    Making every theorem, row by row

    Here's a purely mechanical way to hunt for MU that never misses anything. Start with the axiom. Apply every rule in every possible place to get every string one step away. Then do the same to each of those, and so on. Hofstadter draws this as a tree of theorems. Here, each row lists only the strings that are new: ones that first appear after that many steps.

    Every theorem shows up in some row, eventually. So if MU is a theorem, this search will find it. But if MU isn't a theorem, the search just runs forever, and at no point does it say so. By row 8 (rows 0 to 8) there are already 15,885 theorems, none of them MU, and row 9 alone holds 194,220 more. "Not found yet" is not the same as "never".

    7

    Decision procedures: knowing when to stop

    Definition. A decision procedure is a step-by-step test that is guaranteed to finish and answer yes or no. For example, "is this number even? Look at its last digit" always finishes. A test that might run forever doesn't count.

    The tree search isn't a decision procedure: when the answer is "no", it never finishes. Could we stop it early? Say, "MU has only 2 letters, so once the rows contain only long strings, give up"? That would work if every rule made strings longer. But rules III and IV make strings shorter. A short theorem might be reached only through a detour into long strings, and nothing in the rules limits how long the detour can be.

    The detour to MUII: up the mountain and back down

    MUII has just 4 letters, but its shortest derivation takes 7 steps and climbs to 10 letters on the way (found by searching every derivation, by computer). Short strings can hide deep in the tree.

    Hofstadter notes that a formal system must at least let you recognise its axioms by a sure test, but its theorems need not come with one. For MIU, nothing about the rules tells you in advance whether such a test exists. Finding one takes I-mode thinking.

    8

    The answer: step outside and count

    Hofstadter leaves the puzzle open in Chapter I and gives the answer much later (in Chapter IX). If you'd like to keep trying, stop here.

    Show the answerSpoiler: whether MU can be made, and why.Click to hide.
    1Watch only the number of I's

    Forget the U's and look at one thing: how many I's the string has. Here is what each rule does to that count:

    RuleWhat it does to the I's
    I · add Uno change
    II · doubledoubles the count
    III · III → Utakes away 3
    IV · drop UUno change

    The axiom MI has 1 I. MU has 0.

    2The count can never become a multiple of 3

    Start with 1, which is not a multiple of 3 (0, 3, 6, 9, …). Now check both moves that change the count:

    • Doubling a number that isn't a multiple of 3 never gives one. Look at the remainder after dividing by 3. If it's 1 (like 4), doubling makes it 2 (8 is 6 + 2). If it's 2 (like 5), the remainder doubles to 4, which is 3 + 1, so it's 1 again (10 is 9 + 1). It never lands on 0.
    • Taking away 3 doesn't change the remainder at all: 7 and 4 both leave 1.

    So no matter which rules you use, in any order, the number of I's is never a multiple of 3. And 0 is a multiple of 3. MU can't be made. A property like this, which no step can change, is called an invariant. In a picture: the remainder starts at 1, doubling swaps 1 and 2, taking away 3 leaves it where it is, and nothing ever leads to 0.

    IntuitionNo amount of rule-following inside the system could ever show that MU is impossible. The proof came from outside: we turned strings into numbers (their I-counts) and reasoned about the numbers. Hofstadter later does exactly this to the MIU-system, giving each string a number, so that "is MU a theorem?" becomes a question about numbers. That's the same trick as Gödel numbering, from the Introduction.
    3Bonus: a real decision procedure for MIU

    The count gives more than a no for MU. It turns out a string is a theorem exactly when it is an M followed only by I's and U's, and its number of I's is not a multiple of 3. (The count argument shows nothing else can be made. Showing that every such string really can be made takes more work. It's true, and a computer check of every string up to 9 letters agrees.)     9

    Recap: the dialogue and chapter in one breath

    Followed step by step from inside, Zeno's race and the MU-puzzle both go on forever. Stepped back from, both are settled at once: the steps add up to 10 seconds, and the count of I's is never a multiple of 3.

    Dialogue

    Three voices, one idea

    Zeno meets the characters he invented, and his race begins with "Go!". From inside his argument it never ends; from outside, Achilles catches up after 10 seconds.

    Formal systems

    Rules, not meanings

    An axiom, rules of production, and theorems that are just strings. Making every theorem row by row misses nothing, but never says "no".

    Outside the system

    Noticing is the trick

    M-mode follows rules; I-mode notices patterns, like the I-count. That's how we know MU can't be made, and how we get a test that always finishes.

    10

    Modern view: what we know now

    As of November 2026

    The difference between following rules and stepping back to reason about them is now a practical question: we build machines that do both. Here is where this page's ideas stand today.

    AI · formal proof

    AI that searches the tree of theorems, with a mechanical referee

    In the book: a formal system's theorems form a huge tree. Mechanically checking a step is easy; knowing which step to take is the hard part.

    Now: AI provers work in just this setting. They write proofs in Lean, a proof assistant that checks every step by machine, the way you can check every line of an MIU derivation. Google DeepMind's AlphaProof (published in Nature, November 2025) learned by trial and error, on millions of practice problems, which rules to try. At the 2024 International Mathematical Olympiad it solved 3 of the 5 non-geometry problems, including the hardest. Its successor, AlphaProof Nexus (Science, October 2026), pairs a large language model that proposes proofs with Lean checking them. It settled 9 open problems posed by the mathematician Paul Erdős, two of them open since 1970. The caveats: the Olympiad problems were translated into Lean by people, and Nexus only tried problems that were already written out in Lean.

    Why it matters: it's the M-mode/I-mode split, built into a machine. A learned guesser decides where to look (I-mode-like); a rigid checker makes sure every step is legal (pure M-mode).

    AI · impossible problems

    Do AI models notice when a puzzle can't be solved?

    In the book: a mechanical worker grinds on forever; an intelligent one steps back and suspects the goal is impossible.

    Now: mostly, not yet, and it's disputed. A 2025 study from Apple, "The Illusion of Thinking", reported that AI "reasoning" models (ones trained to write out long step-by-step reasoning before answering) collapse on harder puzzles like Tower of Hanoi and river-crossing problems. Critics replied that some of its river-crossing puzzles were impossible: the boat was too small, so no solution existed. The models had been marked as failing puzzles that had no solution, a trap the test's own authors fell into. A separate 2025 test set, ReliableMath, found that models rarely say "this problem can't be solved". They tend to produce long, confident reasoning instead. We found no published study testing AI models on the MU-puzzle itself.

    Why it matters: recognising that a goal is unreachable is exactly the "jump out of the system" Hofstadter describes, and it's still one of the weak points of today's models. People can miss it too.

    Mathematics · decision procedures

    No test works for every rule game, and the smallest cases are already hard

    In the book: theorems need not come with a decision procedure, and for MIU it takes insight to find one.

    Now: in 1947, Emil Post and Andrey Markov independently proved that no decision procedure works for all rule games of MIU's kind, where rules rewrite parts of strings. Each game may need its own insight, and some have no test at all. The same wall shows up in the "Busy Beaver" hunt, which asks how long a tiny computer program, a Turing machine, can run before stopping. Machines are sized by their number of states (roughly, the rows of their rule table). In 2024 an online collaboration proved that the longest a 5-state machine can run and still stop is 47,176,870 steps. To do it they settled, for all 181 million or so such machines, whether each one stops, and checked the whole proof by machine. For 6 states the problem is open: one 6-state machine, nicknamed Antihydra, stops only if a certain unsolved number puzzle (a cousin of the famous 3n + 1 problem) comes out a particular way, so deciding whether it stops would mean solving that puzzle.

    Why it matters: the gap between "search until you find it" and "know when to give up" from section 7 is a real, permanent limit on computation, not just a feature of one toy.

    AI · interpretability

    Finding the hidden count

    In the book: the MU-puzzle falls to one property the rules never change: the number of I's is never a multiple of 3.

    Now: a property that every step preserves is called an invariant, and finding invariants is central to checking that computer programs are correct. Language models now propose invariants for program loops, and a mechanical checker confirms or rejects them. One 2025 system did this for all 133 programs in a standard test set, against a previous best of 107. Inside AI models, mechanistic interpretability has found similar hidden structure. A small model trained on "clock arithmetic" (adding numbers and keeping only the remainder after dividing by 113) first memorised its examples, then suddenly generalised. Researchers looking inside found it had learned to treat the numbers as positions on a circle, turning addition into rotation. As with MU, it was people looking from outside who found that structure; the model couldn't report it.

    Why it matters: "watch one property the rules can't change" is the I-mode move of section 8, and both machines and the people studying them now make it.
