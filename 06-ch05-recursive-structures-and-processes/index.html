Achilles and the Tortoise read a story about themselves, and the pair in that story step into a picture, where they read another story. They climb back out of all but one of these. This page covers the dialogue Little Harmonic Labyrinth and Chapter V, which gives the pattern its name, recursion: things nested inside things of the same kind. Hofstadter finds it in music, in sentences, in number sequences, in a graph from his own physics research and in the particles matter is made of.

1

A story inside a picture inside a story

Achilles and the Tortoise, the two friends from the earlier dialogues, are riding a Ferris wheel when a helicopter carries them off to the hideaway of a villain named Goodfortune. He leaves them alone in his study while he sharpens his knives; he plans to bake the Tortoise in a pie. On the desk lies a book of adventures of Achilles and the Tortoise. They pass the time by reading one aloud, each taking his own part.

From here on the dialogue keeps going down. The pair in the book drink a "pushing-potion" that drops them inside a picture, a print by M. C. Escher, the Dutch artist from the Introduction. The print, Convex and Concave, shows two worlds that each make sense alone and can't both be real, a picture of the previous page's point that a system can have internal consistency and still clash with a second one. To get back out you drink "popping-tonic": one sip undoes one push. Inside the picture they find another book about themselves, and read from it too.

Step through the story on the right. Each step down is a world entered and each step up is a world left. The list beside the map holds the worlds that are waiting for the pair to come back.

What to noticeThree steps go down and only two come up. The dialogue ends one level short of where it began, with the first Achilles and Tortoise still waiting for Goodfortune and his knives. Its last two words, "Good Fortune", name the villain it never went back to. (That the words are a wink is our reading; Hofstadter says only that the first tension is never resolved.)

Waiting to be resumed

One scene inside the picture replays two ideas from the Introduction. Achilles rubs a lamp and a Genie offers him three wishes. He asks for a hundred wishes instead. That is a wish about wishes, a "meta-wish", and the Genie may not grant it without permission from the Meta-Genie one level above, who must ask the Meta-Meta-Genie, and so on up without end. This is the strict ladder of levels that Russell and Whitehead built to keep paradox out of mathematics: a wish may only be about things on lower rungs. Each genie asks in half the time the one below took, so the whole endless tower answers within a moment: ½ + ¼ + ⅛ + … never passes 1. (It is the sum behind Zeno's paradoxes, the ancient puzzles about endlessly many steps fitting into a finite stretch.) Achilles asks who sits at the top. Nobody does. The authority the genies call GOD is an acronym, a word made from the first letters of a phrase. The phrase is "GOD Over Djinn", whose first word is the acronym itself, so you can never finish spelling it out.

The tower grants Achilles one wish free of the ladder's rule. He at once wishes that his wish not be granted. To grant it is to refuse it and to refuse it is to grant it. This is the liar's sentence "this statement is false" from the Introduction, turned into a wish. The universe survives, but the world of the picture stops like a crashed computer. When it starts up again the pair are inside a different Escher print. They have come back at the right depth, in the wrong place.

2

The form: a piece that ends in the wrong key

The dialogue borrows its title from a short organ piece, the Little Harmonic Labyrinth, long printed under Bach's name (many scholars now doubt he wrote it). The piece is a maze of key changes.

A reminder from the Introduction: a key is a home note plus the family of notes a piece mostly uses, and a modulation is a move to a new key. Musicians call the home note the tonic. Music wanders away from the tonic, which builds tension, and comes back to it, which releases the tension. That release is called a resolution.

At the deepest level of the story, Achilles and the Tortoise are tiny and walking along the groove of a record of this piece. The Tortoise explains that a modulation sets up a second goal. You want to hear the new key's home note soon, and at the back of your mind you still want the original one. The music then lands firmly on the new home note. Achilles hears a grand ending. The Tortoise knows it is the wrong key, and that whoever made the record was fooled as well and cut the piece off there.

The sketch here does the same thing in a few bars. It starts in the key of C, moves to the key of G and comes to a full stop on G. Play the cut-off record first and ask yourself whether it sounds finished. If it does, you heard it as Achilles did, and that is the trap. Then play the whole piece and listen for the return to C.

An original sketch written for this page, not Bach's notes. Higher bars are higher notes; time runs left to right.

The trickThe dialogue is built like the record. Goodfortune's hideaway is the home key. Each story-within-a-story is a move to a new key. The cosy arrival at the Tortoise's house sounds like an ending, but it is an ending one level down, and the dialogue stops there, in the wrong key. The names join in the game: the story they read is called "Djinn and Tonic", and the tonic is both the drink that pops you home and the home note.

The printed dialogue even shows its levels: each story-within-a-story is set further in from the left margin. Chapter V now gives names to what the dialogue acted out.

Push, pop and stack

Hofstadter's word for what the dialogue did is recursion: nesting, and variations on nesting. Stories inside stories, Russian dolls inside Russian dolls, a remark in brackets (inside another remark (in brackets)). The page on Chapter III used the word "recursive" in a different way; section 10 shows how the two uses connect.

The everyday version is putting one task on hold to do another of the same kind. Hofstadter's example is an executive on the phone. He is talking to A when B rings, so he puts A on hold. While he talks to B, C rings, and B goes on hold too. When C hangs up he goes back to B, and when B hangs up, back to A.

Computer scientists have three words for this.

Push: set aside what you are doing, without forgetting where you were, and start a new task one level down.
Pop: finish the task on your current level and pick up the one above, exactly where you left it.
Stack: the record that makes popping possible. For each unfinished task it holds two things: where you were in it, and what you needed to know at that point.

The names come from the spring-loaded pile of trays in a cafeteria. A tray pushed on top sinks the pile a little, and the tray you take off is always the one put on last.

The list beside the story map in section 1 was a stack. Pushing-potion and reading a story are pushes. Popping-tonic, the popcorn and the climb "up one story" are pops. Four things the dialogue did now have plain descriptions:

  • The wrong print. A stack restores what you needed to know. After the crash it restored only part: "an Escher picture with lizards", but not which one.
  • The wrong house. The pair lost their tonic and left the print by climbing out, not by popping. One way to read the ending: with no proper pop, nothing sent them back to the spot they had left, so they surface on the right level in the wrong place.
  • The unpopped push. The first story was pushed and never popped. In Hofstadter's own diagram of the dialogue, which has the shape of the story map, the line never climbs back to Goodfortune's hideaway.
  • Why it is hard to follow. A radio news report can go three levels down (studio, foreign correspondent, a taped interview she plays) and nobody gets lost, because each level sounds different. In the dialogue the same two characters appear on every level, so you have to keep the stack in your head on purpose.
♪Stacks in music

The dialogue hints that we listen with a stack of keys: each modulation pushes a key, and we want them popped in reverse order until the tonic returns. Hofstadter calls this an exaggeration with a grain of truth. What most listeners do keep is a stack just two deep: the true home key, and the key the music is in right now. That is enough to tell a local arrival from the real one. In the sketch in section 2, the row under the notes shows that short stack. When the music stops on G, the key of C is still in it.

A false ending, he adds, should raise the overall tension for a listener who remembers the home key, as a cosy scene does in a thriller when you know the villain is still out there. His example is a dance by Bach, the gigue from the French Suite no. 5. Its first half moves from G to D and stops there, sounding like an ending. Its second half starts in D and comes home to G. Past two levels our ears give up. Bach's Labyrinth changes key so fast that a listener loses track of where home was, and the Endlessly Rising Canon from the Introduction climbs through six keys and arrives "home" an octave too high without most listeners noticing.

4

Recursion in language: sentence machines

Sentences nest too, and our stack for them is only a little deeper than our stack for keys. Each line below pushes one more clause into the middle of the one before:

  • The record stopped.
  • The record that the needle scraped stopped.
  • The record that the needle that the Tortoise held scraped stopped.
  • The record that the needle that the Tortoise that the Genie startled held scraped stopped.

The last one is correct English and almost unreadable: three verbs arrive in a row, and each has to be handed back to a noun you set aside earlier. Hofstadter's example is German, where verbs often wait until the end of the sentence, and the old jokes about professors who finish a long sentence by rattling off a string of them.

A grammar is the set of rules for which arrangements of words count as sentences. The chapter draws a piece of grammar as a little map of pathways, a recursive transition network, or RTN. You enter at one end and follow the arrows to the other, and each box you pass (the chapter calls a box a node) tells you what to say next.

The kinds of word the boxes ask for:
article: the, a · adjective: strange, purple, small · noun: bagels, cow, horns
preposition: without, near, under · relative pronoun: that, which · verb: gobbled, chased

The first network is called ORNATE NOUN. Its path is: an article if you like, then as many adjectives as you like, then a noun. It makes "bagels", "the cow", "the strange small bagels".

A box may also hold the name of a whole network. Meeting that box means: go and run that network, then come back here. This is a procedure call, and it is a push; reaching the far end of the called network is the pop.

The second network, FANCY NOUN, has four pathways. All of them call ORNATE NOUN. Three of them also call FANCY NOUN itself.

Is that a circle?A fancy noun is defined using fancy nouns. That is a recursive definition, and it looks circular. It isn't, because one pathway doesn't call FANCY NOUN. Take that pathway and the calls stop. Hofstadter's phrase for this is that the definition bottoms out: a recursive definition describes a thing by way of simpler versions of the same thing, not by way of itself.

Build a fancy noun. Each time FANCY NOUN is called, you choose its pathway. Try to make the stack grow, then find the only way to make it shrink.

Stack: calls not yet finished

If you always choose a pathway that calls FANCY NOUN again, the phrase never ends, just as "GOD Over Djinn" can never be fully spelled out. A recursion finishes only if, sooner or later, it takes a way out.

Recursion can also be indirect. A network for a clause might call FANCY NOUN when it needs an object for its verb, while FANCY NOUN calls the clause network in turn. Two things that each make the other recall Escher's Drawing Hands from the Introduction, but here nothing is paradoxical: the pair still finish, as long as some pathway bottoms out. When a whole family of procedures call one another with none of them on top, the arrangement is called a heterarchy, in contrast to a hierarchy, where the levels stay in order.

5

Recursion with numbers: Diagram G, Fibonacci and a wild sequence

A network that calls itself can describe a picture as well as a phrase. Hofstadter defines a tree he calls Diagram G in one small drawing: a dot with two branches growing up from it. At the end of the left branch sits the letter G. The right branch has one extra dot, and then a G. Each G stands for a complete copy of the whole drawing.

Click a G to replace it with what it stands for. Every G you open gives you two more.

Structures and processesThis looks like the endless spelling-out of GOD, and for the picture it is: Diagram G is infinite and can never be drawn whole. That is allowed. A structure may go on for ever, because you only open a G when you get to it. A process that is meant to finish, such as building one phrase or computing one number, is the thing that must bottom out.

Now number the dots from the bottom up, left to right. The numbers on the right-hand edge are 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, …: the Fibonacci numbers, in which each number is the sum of the two before it, starting from 1 and 1. They are named after Leonardo of Pisa, called Fibonacci, who described them around 1202.

The Fibonacci rule is itself a recursive definition, with two parts:

FIBO(n) = FIBO(n − 1) + FIBO(n − 2)the rule, for n above 2
FIBO(1) = FIBO(2) = 1the bottom

Read "FIBO(n)" as "the n-th Fibonacci number". To find the 5th you need the 4th and the 3rd, for those you need earlier ones, and the questions stop when they reach the 1st and 2nd, which are given outright. The process bottoms out. (You can also skip the questions and work forwards from the bottom, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, …, each number made from the two already in hand. Section 10 comes back to this.)

Hofstadter found Diagram G by going the other way, from a rule to the tree. The rule is odder than Fibonacci's because it is nested inside itself:

G(n) = n − G(G(n − 1))the rule, for n above 0
G(0) = 0the bottom
By hand, each line using the lines before it:
G(1) = 1 − G(G(0)) = 1 − G(0) = 1
G(2) = 2 − G(G(1)) = 2 − G(1) = 1
G(3) = 3 − G(G(2)) = 3 − G(1) = 2
G(4) = 4 − G(G(3)) = 4 − G(2) = 3
G(5) = 5 − G(G(4)) = 5 − G(3) = 3

Put each number n on top of the number G(n) and you have built the tree: 4 and 5 both sit on 3, and 3 sits on 2. The numbered diagram above checks this for every dot it shows. A recursive picture and a recursive formula turn out to be the same thing. Add one more layer of nesting to the formula, H(n) = n − H(H(H(n − 1))), and you get a Diagram H whose right branch has one more dot. He also gives a pair of rules, F and M, each defined by way of the other, and calls them "married".

QA tidy rule with a wild result

Hofstadter then changes the Fibonacci rule slightly. Each new number is still the sum of two earlier ones, but not the last two. The last two numbers say how far back to count to find the pair to add.

Q(n) = Q(n − Q(n − 1)) + Q(n − Q(n − 2))for n above 2
Q(1) = Q(2) = 1

The sequence starts 1, 1, 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 8, 8, 8, 10, 9, 10. Step through it, then run it on. Fibonacci numbers grow smoothly. The Q-numbers stay near half of n, the dashed line, but they jump above and below it with no pattern anyone has found. Hofstadter asked whether the disorder hides some regularity. The rule is a perfectly orderly one and, as he puts it, it produces chaos.

Q(n)half of n

A rule needs a bottom: two graphs made of copies of themselves

Every recursive definition so far had two lines: the rule, and the bottom. The bottom matters as much as the rule. Keep Fibonacci's rule and change the starting pair from 1, 1 to 1, 3, and you get a different sequence, the Lucas numbers:

BottomSame rule: add the last two
1, 1
1, 3

Hofstadter shows the same thing with a graph from his own number research, which he calls INT. Look closely at any piece of it and you find a small, bent copy of the whole graph, and inside that copy more copies, without end. "It is made of copies of itself" sounds like a full description. It is half of one. The other half says where the copies sit and how each is bent. He draws that half as a picture of empty boxes and calls it the skeleton. To build the graph, put a small copy of the skeleton into each of its own boxes, then do the same in every new box, and so on.

The skeleton is the bottom. Change it, keep the nesting rule, and a different graph appears. On the right are two simple skeletons of our own (not the book's). Nest each a few times.

Give INT's rule a different, more complicated skeleton and you get the main graph of Hofstadter's doctoral thesis in physics, which he calls Gplot. It answers an idealized question: which energies can an electron have inside a crystal that sits in a magnetic field?

Each horizontal slice of the graph is one strength of field, written as a fraction between 0 and 1. (The fraction compares two rhythms of the electron's motion, one set by the crystal and one by the field.) Left to right is energy. The dark stretches in a slice are the energies the electron is allowed; the gaps are forbidden.

Click anywhere on the graph to zoom in. Smaller, skewed copies of the whole shape keep turning up, at every size.

Hofstadter wrote that he would be the most surprised person in the world if this graph ever came out of an experiment. He valued it as mathematics that showed how to treat less idealized problems.

7

Recursion at the bottom of matter

The chapter's boldest example comes from particle physics. It needs a few words first.

Electron: the light, electrically charged particle found in every atom. Photon: a particle of light. Positron: the electron's mirror twin, alike except for the opposite charge.
Bare particle: a particle as it would be if it never affected any other. No such thing exists; it is a starting point for calculation.
Virtual particle: one that flickers into being and vanishes again before it could be seen.
Feynman diagram: a sketch of one way particles can meet, split and rejoin, named after the physicist Richard Feynman. Each sketch stands for a piece of a calculation.

Picture a bare electron travelling from A to B: a straight line. A real electron does more on the way. It can send out a virtual photon and catch it again. It can do so twice, or send one out while another is still in flight, a loop inside a loop. And any photon can turn for an instant into an electron and a positron, which meet and become the photon again. Each of those can do all of the same things in turn, to any depth.

So a real electron is a bare electron wrapped in a cloud of virtual particles, each wrapped in a cloud of its own. Physicists call the real one renormalized. No particle can be described without the others, whose descriptions depend on it in turn, like Hofstadter's married rules F and M in section 5.

Not every drawing is allowed. The laws of physics, such as the rule that electric charge is never created or destroyed, pick out the ones that are, the way a grammar picks out sentences. An illegal drawing is not well-formed, the word Chapter II used for strings of the right shape. And this grammar is recursive: it lets a legal piece be nested inside a legal piece. Hofstadter remarks that one could draw RTNs for it.

To predict what a real electron does, a physicist has to add up the contributions of infinitely many drawings. Nobody knows how to find the total, unlike Zeno's sum in section 1. Nature offers no bottom here; the physicist supplies one by stopping. What saves the method is that the more complicated a drawing, the less it contributes, so the sum can be stopped early. Hofstadter reports that keeping roughly the hundred simplest drawings had given one measured quantity correctly to nine decimal places.

8

When are two things the same?

Hofstadter returns to Gplot to ask what "copy" means, and climbs four rungs, each looser than the last.

  1. Exact rule. The Introduction's canons copy a tune upside down, backwards or at half speed. Each copy keeps all the information in the tune; the chapter's word for such a match is isomorphism.
  2. Bent copies. The copies inside Gplot are squeezed, skewed and flipped, and the eye still says "the same shape".
  3. Same working parts. In Escher's wood engraving Butterflies no two butterflies match line for line, but wing answers to wing and body to body. In his woodcut Fishes and Scales each scale of a fish is a small fish. Real scales are not copies of the fish, but every cell of a real fish does carry its DNA, the fish's chemical building plan, which Hofstadter counts as a copy of the whole fish in a heavily disguised form.
  4. Style. No part of one Escher print matches a part of another, yet a small piece of any of them gives the artist away. The same is true of a few bars of Bach.
Why this belongs in a chapter on recursionRecursion is the same thing happening on several levels at once, and "same" never means identical. The stories in the dialogue share two facts only: each is a story, and each has Achilles and the Tortoise in it. Hofstadter says the question "when are two things the same?" will return throughout the book, and that in the end it is tied to the nature of intelligence.
9

Recursion in programs

Seeing that two jobs are the same, apart from some detail, is the central skill of programming, because it lets one piece of program serve for both. Hofstadter names three ways to use it.

A loop is a set of steps done over and over until some condition is met. To test whether N is prime, try dividing it by 2, then 3, then 4, and stop when something divides it or you reach N − 1. Some loops have a known maximum number of rounds; others just run until the condition turns up, and may run for ever. Hofstadter calls these bounded and free loops, and Chapter XIII is about the difference. Loops nest inside loops, as in knitting.

A procedure is a group of steps given a name, like ORNATE NOUN, so that it can be called from anywhere. It can be handed a value to work with, called a parameter, and it can choose its pathway by looking at that value.

The third is a procedure that calls itself. A chess program defines its best move as the one that leaves the opponent with the worst best reply. Finding the opponent's best reply is the same question from the other side, so the procedure calls itself, playing the opponent. To make it bottom out, a parameter says how many moves to look ahead, and each call passes on a number one smaller. At zero the procedure stops asking and just scores the position, for example by counting pieces.

A look-ahead tree, drawn here with two choices per move. The parameter falls by 1 on each level. At 0 the positions are scored outright, and the scores are passed back up.

Hofstadter notes that strong human players look ahead much less than chess programs did, and in 1979 still played far better. Early forecasts had put a computer world champion ten years off; ten years later it still seemed more than ten years off. This gave him a law that calls itself: Hofstadter's Law, "It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law."

10

The two meanings of "recursive" meet

The page on Chapter III met the phrase recursively enumerable: a set is recursively enumerable when some formal system can list its members, starting from axioms and applying rules again and again. Each new member is made out of earlier ones, as each MIU string in Chapter I was made from a string already in hand. Hofstadter pictures a snowball. It is the forwards way of working from section 5. That is recursion in this chapter's sense: new things defined by way of simpler things of the same kind, from a bottom that is given outright. The Fibonacci numbers and the Lucas numbers are sets of this sort, each growing from two starting numbers under one rule. (Chapter III called a set "recursive" when the things outside it can be listed this way as well.)

The Q-sequence showed that things built by fixed rules can still surprise. Hofstadter closes on that thought. Perhaps a rich enough recursive system could break out of any pattern laid down for it in advance, and that is one mark of intelligence. He suggests going one step past procedures that call themselves, to programs that work on programs: extending, repairing and improving them, themselves included. He calls this "tangled recursion" and guesses that it lies at the heart of intelligence. Plain recursion keeps its levels in order: you go down, and you come back up the way you went. In the Introduction's strange loop, moving through the levels lands you back where you began. A program that rewrites the program it is part of has that tangled shape.

11

Recap: the dialogue and chapter in one breath

Recursion is a thing built from simpler copies of itself. A stack keeps track of the copies you are inside, and a bottom that is given outright stops the definition from going round in a circle.

Dialogue

Three pushes, two pops

Stories nest inside pictures inside stories. The outermost one is never resumed, as a record cut off at a false ending never returns to its home key.

Definitions

A rule and a bottom

FANCY NOUN, Fibonacci and G(n) are each defined by way of themselves, and each has a way out. Change the bottom and you change the result. A tidy rule can still give the disorder of the Q-sequence.

The world

Copies that are not identical

Gplot is made of bent copies of itself, and a real particle is wrapped in clouds of particles. Seeing what is "the same" across levels is the skill behind programs, and maybe behind minds.
