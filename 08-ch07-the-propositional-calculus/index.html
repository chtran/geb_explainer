Dialogue
Chromatic Fantasy, And Feud
1

A green shell that is not green

The story. The Tortoise, fresh from a wash in a pond, tells Achilles to admire how green his clean shell is. Achilles agrees that it is green, and the Tortoise replies that it is not. Achilles thinks he has caught him in a contradiction, which he defines as saying a thing and denying it in one sentence. The Tortoise points out that he used two sentences, so by that definition he is in the clear. It is Achilles whose thinking is inconsistent, he adds, and Achilles cannot see it from inside his own tangle. From outside it is as plain as day.

So Achilles tries to make two sentences into one: if you believe each of two sentences, surely you believe them combined? The Tortoise agrees, provided the way of combining is one everybody accepts, and offers his own. "Politicians lie" is true, and so is "Cast-iron sinks" (cast iron does sink); joined with "in" they give "Politicians lie in cast-iron sinks", which is not true. Combining is clearly risky. Achilles protests that the right word is "and", and that joining true sentences with "and" is harmless. If it is harmless, asks the Tortoise, why is Achilles trying so hard to make him do it? Achilles ends up apologising, and the Tortoise leaves without ever agreeing to "my shell is green and my shell is not green".

The title

Bach wrote a keyboard piece called the Chromatic Fantasy and Fugue. A fantasy, in music, is a free piece that sounds made up on the spot. A fugue is the opposite of free: a form with strict rules.

Hofstadter says himself that the dialogue copies almost nothing from Bach's piece except its title. The title is a pun. "Fugue" has become "Feud", and the feud is over the word "And". "Chromatic" comes from the Greek word for colour, and the quarrel starts with the colour of a shell. (That last reading is ours, not the book's.)

To hear what "chromatic" means, here is a run that takes every piano key in turn, up and back down. (An original sketch, not Bach's notes.)

The book has staged this before. In Lewis Carroll's dialogue (Chapter II (page 03)) the Tortoise accepted "if A and B, then Z", accepted A and B, and still refused Z. There the stubborn word was "if… then". Here it is "and".

The idea the dialogue sets upThe test hiding in the Tortoise's example is a good one: a way of combining sentences must never turn true sentences into a false one. "And" passes and his "in" does not. But a word has no power to make anyone use it properly, and the Tortoise drops the ordinary meaning of "and" the moment it would trap him. So the chapter builds a formal system in which one symbol does exactly what Achilles wished "and" would do, and three more do the same for "not", "or" and "if… then". Symbols in a formal system cannot wriggle, because the rules are all they have. (Whether that finally answers the Tortoise is a question for section 8. His jibe about seeing a muddle only from outside comes back in sections 9 and 10.)
Chapter VII
The Propositional Calculus
2

The symbols, and which strings count

A lot of everyday reasoning depends only on how four small words are used: "and", "or", "not" and "if… then". From "it is raining and it is cold" you may conclude "it is cold", whatever the weather is actually like. Hofstadter calls reasoning of this kind propositional reasoning (a proposition is a statement that is either true or false). The Propositional Calculus is a formal system built to imitate it. "Calculus" here just means a method of calculating with symbols; it has nothing to do with the calculus taught in school.

Like the systems on earlier pages, it is a game played with strings. Hofstadter gives the symbols and rules first and says what they stand for only afterwards. We do the same. For now the symbols are only shapes, and you can try to work out from the rules which shape plays which of the four words.

The alphabet
<>PQR′∧∨⊃~[]

The symbol ~ is called a tilde. The square brackets are kept for section 4. They are used to mark off parts of the working and are never part of a string.

Not every row of these symbols is allowed into the game. As with the pq-system on page 03, there is a test of shape that comes before anything else: a string must be well-formed. The well-formed strings are built up from the smallest ones.

Atoms

P, Q and R are well-formed. So is any of them followed by one or more of the marks ′, called primes (nothing to do with prime numbers): Q′, R′′. The primes are there so the supply never runs out. These simplest strings are called atoms.

Formation rules

If x and y are well-formed, so are these four strings:

~x   <x∧y>   <x∨y>   <x⊃y>

Here x and y are not symbols of the system. They stand for whole well-formed strings.

Start with atoms and apply the formation rules again and again, and you can build strings as long as you like. A definition like this, where big cases are built out of smaller cases of the same thing, is called recursive, the subject of Chapter V (page 06). From P and Q′: first ~Q′, then <P∧~Q′>, then ~<P∧~Q′>, and so on. Notice that a tilde needs no brackets, but each of ∧, ∨ and ⊃ must come wrapped in its own pair.

To test a string someone hands you, run the rules backwards. Take the string apart into the pieces it must have been made from, then take those apart, until you reach atoms or get stuck. This is a top-down test, and it always finishes: every formation rule makes strings longer, so every step backwards gives shorter pieces, and that cannot go on forever. It is a decision procedure for being well-formed.

3

Three rules, and two symbols to guess

Being well-formed only gets a string into the game. The strings that matter are the theorems. Here are the first three rules. In all of them x and y stand for well-formed strings.

Joining rule

If x and y are theorems, then <x∧y> is a theorem.

Separation rule

If <x∧y> is a theorem, then x and y are both theorems.

Double-tilde rule

The pair ~~ can be deleted from any theorem. It can also be inserted, as long as the result is still well-formed.

Can you guess what ∧ is going to stand for? Two theorems can be joined with it into one theorem, and a theorem made with it can be split back into its two halves. That is exactly how the troublesome word of the dialogue ought to behave: ∧ is the system's "and". The joining rule is the step Achilles could not make the Tortoise take. Here it is a rule of the game, and no player can refuse it.

And the tilde? Two of them in a row cancel out, the way "it is not the case that the shell is not green" just says the shell is green. The tilde is the system's "not".

So far these are guesses, and nothing in the system depends on them. The rules never mention "and" or "not". They only say which shapes may be written after which.

Something is missingEvery formal system on earlier pages started from axioms: strings handed to you as theorems for free. This one has none. But all three rules above need a theorem to start from. With no axioms, how does the first theorem ever get made?
4

The fantasy rule: theorems out of thin air

The answer is one special rule that needs no theorem to start from. Hofstadter calls it the fantasy rule; the word comes back from the dialogue's title. It works like saying "let's pretend".

Write down any well-formed string x you like, and ask: what if x were a theorem? Then use the rules to see what follows. Suppose that after some steps you arrive at a string y. Everything from x to y is the fantasy; x is its premise (the thing supposed) and y is its outcome. Nothing inside the fantasy is a theorem, because it all rests on a pretence. But you have learned something real:

If x were a theorem, y would be a theorem.

The fantasy rule lets you write that discovery down as a string, and this string is a genuine theorem:

<x⊃y>

The square brackets mark where a fantasy starts and stops. A [ means "push into a fantasy; the next line is its premise". A ] means "pop back out; the line before was its outcome". (Push and pop are the book's words, from Chapter V.) Here is the smallest example: the derivation of the chapter's first theorem.

Inside the fantasy we pretended P and the double-tilde rule gave ~~P. Outside, we are left with a real theorem, <P⊃~~P>. Note that P itself is not a theorem. We only asked what would follow if it were.

This gives away the third symbol. <x⊃y> records "if x, then y": ⊃ is the system's "if… then".

aFantasies inside fantasies

Inside a fantasy you can push again and start another one, as a film can contain a film. Hofstadter calls the layers levels of reality. Popping out of an inner fantasy puts you back in the outer one, which is more real than where you were but still not the real world. Only lines at the outermost level, inside no brackets at all, are theorems.

Inside a fantasy, its lines are treated as if they were theorems: that is the pretence. So wherever a rule says "theorem", read "line at the level you are on". Joining, separation and double-tilde all work inside a fantasy on the lines of that fantasy, and so will every rule still to come.

What may cross between levels? The carry-over rule settles it. The rule beside it, detachment, puts to use the "if… then" strings that the fantasy rule makes.

Carry-over rule

Inside a fantasy, any line from the level just outside it can be brought in and used.

Hofstadter's picture: a "No Smoking" sign in a cinema that applies to the actors in the film too. It works inwards only. If lines could be carried out of a fantasy, you could push, write any string at all as a premise, and carry it out as a theorem.
Rule of detachment

If x and <x⊃y> are both theorems, then y is a theorem.

The fantasy rule makes "if… then" strings; this rule uses them. It is the very step Carroll's Tortoise refused on page 03: he granted the "if… then" and its first half, and would not grant the second. Logicians call this rule modus ponens.

What the fantasy rule really does"If x were a theorem, y would be" is a remark made in English, from outside, about the system. The fantasy rule turns that remark into a string of the system, <x⊃y>. An observation about the game becomes a position in the game. Getting a system to hold statements about itself is where the whole book is heading. It is the first turn of the Strange Loop from the Introduction (page 01): step up a level to talk about the system, and you land back inside it.

What the symbols are meant to mean

Here is the interpretation (page 03) this system was designed for.

SymbolRead asNote
∧andAs guessed in section 3.

⊃if… then<x⊃y> reads "if x, then y".
∨or"One or the other, or both." No rule has used it yet; section 6 brings it in.

P, Q, …any sentence you likeAn atom has no fixed meaning. Give it any sentence, and keep to that sentence throughout.

Hofstadter fills the atoms with lines from Zen koans, a mischievous thing to feed to a logic machine. He calls the results "Zentences". Let P be "this mind is Buddha" and Q be "this flax weighs three pounds". Then the three targets of section 4 read:

<P⊃~~P>If this mind is Buddha, then it is not the case that this mind is not Buddha.
<<P∧Q>⊃<Q∧P>>If this mind is Buddha and this flax weighs three pounds, then this flax weighs three pounds and this mind is Buddha.
<P⊃<Q⊃<P∧Q>>>If this mind is Buddha, then, if this flax weighs three pounds, then this mind is Buddha and this flax weighs three pounds.

Each one is true, and so obviously true that it hardly seems worth saying. That is the point. There are plenty of false sentences the system could have produced, such as "this mind is Buddha and this mind is not Buddha", and it produced none of them. Hofstadter compares it to someone crossing a stream on stepping-stones: the system steps from truth to truth and never gets its feet wet. And it does this without anyone inside it thinking about minds or flax. The rules only move shapes. (Three examples prove nothing, of course. Section 7 shows how the claim can be checked.)

Where the meaning comes fromNobody told the system that ∧ means "and". The joining and separation rules make ∧ behave the way "and" behaves, and that behaviour is all the meaning it has. Page 03 called this passive meaning: the symbols mean something to us only because the rules make them act that way. This is the reply to the dialogue. Achilles could not hold the Tortoise to "and". Nobody needs to hold ∧ to anything, provided they agree to play by the rules.

True whatever the atoms say

You do not even need to choose sentences for the atoms. Read only the other symbols, and <P⊃~~P> becomes "if P, then not not P". Hofstadter calls this a semi-interpretation. It is not a sentence, since P is not one, yet it already sounds true, because you can see that it will come out true whichever sentence you drop in for P.

That is the key idea of the Propositional Calculus. Its theorems are the patterns that come out true no matter what sentences fill the atoms.

Hofstadter puts it another way: these are statements that would be true in every conceivable world. That is why they sound empty. They tell you nothing about minds, flax or shells. What they show is the form that such always-true statements have, and that every one of them can be turned out by a single small set of shape-moving rules.

6

The last three rules

Three more rules complete the system. Each says that two shapes are interchangeable: wherever one occurs, as a whole theorem or as a piece inside one, you may swap in the other. Now that the symbols can be read, each rule can be checked against a sentence. Take x as "the shell is green" and y as "the shell is clean".

Contrapositive rule

<x⊃y> and <~y⊃~x> are interchangeable.

"If the shell is green, then it is clean" says the same as "if the shell is not clean, then it is not green".
De Morgan's rule

<~x∧~y> and ~<x∨y> are interchangeable.

"The shell is not green and the shell is not clean" says the same as "it is not true that the shell is green or clean".
Switcheroo rule

<x∨y> and <~x⊃y> are interchangeable.

"The shell is green or it is clean (or both)" says the same as "if the shell is not green, then it is clean".

De Morgan's rule is named after the British logician Augustus De Morgan. "Switcheroo" is Hofstadter's own joke name.

A longer derivation: Ganto's Ax

In one koan the monk Ganto raises an ax over two meditating monks and says: if you say a word, I will cut off your heads; and if you do not say a word, I will also cut off your heads. Let P be "you say a word" and Q be "I will cut off your heads". The threat is the string <<P⊃Q>∧<~P⊃Q>>. What if it were a theorem? Push it as a premise and see.

About two dozen thoughtless steps, and out comes Q: the heads come off. (The book stops at line 24. Line 25 is what the fantasy rule then gives.) Along the way the system used <P∨~P>, "you say a word or you don't", exactly as a person would.

The koan itself ends differently. The monks go on meditating as if nothing had been said, and Ganto puts down the ax and praises them. The logic is flawless, and the koan steps around it. Hofstadter asks, half joking, whether koans too might all come from one set of rules, and whether a mechanical test could tell a real koan from a fake. He takes Zen up properly two chapters later.

7

Every possible world: a test that always finishes

Chapter I (page 02) met the MIU-system, a letter game whose theorems are strings such as MI and MIU, and asked: is there a decision procedure for theorems? Making theorems one after another can tell you "yes" if your string turns up, but it can never tell you "no".

For the Propositional Calculus there is such a test. Logicians have proved that a well-formed string is a theorem of the Propositional Calculus exactly when it is true in every world, in the sense explained below. (The book implies as much, without proof.) Hofstadter names it, the method of truth tables, and leaves the details to logic textbooks. It follows so directly from section 5's idea, that theorems are true whatever the atoms say, that we show it here. (This demo goes one step beyond the book.)

The idea: each atom stands for a sentence, and a sentence is either true or false. So for a string with two atoms there are only four cases to consider, however many sentences there are in the world: both true, first only, second only, neither. Call each case a world. In each world, work out whether the whole string is true, using the meanings of the symbols:

~xis true when x is false.
<x∧y>is true when both are true.
<x∨y>is true when at least one is true.
<x⊃y>is false in just one case: x true and y false. Otherwise it is true.

The last line surprises people. Think of a promise: "if it rains, I will bring an umbrella". The only way to break it is for it to rain and for me to turn up without one. On a dry day I have kept my word whatever I carry. So when x is false, <x⊃y> counts as true.

Work from the inside out. Take <<P⊃Q>⊃<Q⊃P>> in the world where P is false and Q is true. The left piece <P⊃Q> is true, because its first half is false. The right piece <Q⊃P> is false, because Q is true and P is false. So the whole string has the form "true ⊃ false", the one case where ⊃ comes out false.

Pick a string, then flip the atoms between true and false and watch the sentence. The table has a column for each piece of the string, so you can check every step.

String:
All the worlds at once

Try P, then ~P, then <P∨~P>. "P or not P" is a theorem, yet P is not, and ~P is not either.

The first four strings are theorems you have seen derived, and they come out true in every row. The other five fail in at least one. Notice that the tables are not part of the system. They are a way of reasoning about it from outside.

So to decide whether a string is a theorem you never need to hunt for a derivation. Fill in its table: 2 rows for one atom, 4 for two, 8 for three, doubling with each new atom, but always a finite number. Making theorems one after another is what Chapter III (page 04) called listing them: a set that can be listed this way is recursively enumerable. Page 04 asked whether the non-theorems of a system can always be listed too. For this system the answer is yes: the table is a sure test for being in, and for being out.

8

Can we be sure it never goes wrong?

Section 7 said logicians have proved that every theorem comes out true in every world. If x is true in every world then ~x is false in every world, so the two can never both be theorems. A system like that, which never produces a string and its opposite, is consistent. The proof exists. But did we need it in order to be sure, and how much does it add? Hofstadter stages the question as an argument between two voices.

Prudence

We only know it because it has been proved. Perhaps some rule is subtly wrong and, far down the line, both x and ~x turn up as theorems.

Imprudence

Look at the rules. Each one plainly does what "and", "not" or "if… then" does. If you doubt that, you doubt your own reasoning. And any proof you gave would itself use "and", "not" and "if… then", in arguments far more tangled than these nine rules. What would it add? It would be like a dictionary that defines simple words by means of complicated ones.

One exchange is sharper. Imprudence asks why two theorems x and ~x should be worse than two theorems x and xU in the MIU-system of page 02. Prudence replies that MI and MIU, both theorems there, do not contradict each other. Imprudence answers that x and ~x clash only if you read ~ as "not", and it is the rules that make us read it so. So Prudence already trusts that the rules capture those words, which is the very thing Prudence wanted proved. (As Chapter IV (page 05) put it, consistency belongs to a system together with an interpretation.)

The egg and the boxesHofstadter compares a system of reasoning to an egg. Its shell protects it, and to ship it you pack it in a box, and for safety put that box inside another. Each proof that your reasoning is sound (never leads from true to false) is one more box. However many you add, you can imagine a disaster big enough to break the egg. You ship it anyway. In the same way there is never a final proof that a proof is correct: you can prove the proof, and prove that proof, but the outermost layer is always taken on trust. Mathematicians do not usually lose sleep over this, Hofstadter says, but when a proof is very long, or made by a computer, people do start to ask what "proved" really means.

This is Carroll's dialogue once more. There, each time Achilles stated a rule of reasoning, the Tortoise wrote it down as one more sentence to be accepted, and asked for a rule to get past that one. Hofstadter suggests writing their quarrel in the new notation. Achilles says: if you have <<A∧B>⊃Z> and you have <A∧B>, then you have Z. The Tortoise writes the rule down as a string, putting ∧ for its "and" and ⊃ for its "if… then", and asks: you mean this?

Achilles' rule, written as a string<<<<A∧B>⊃Z>∧<A∧B>>⊃Z>

(A, B and Z are borrowed from Carroll's dialogue; they are not atoms of our system. Page 03, section 2, lets you play the whole exchange out.)

Now there are three strings on the table and still no Z. If Achilles states a rule for getting Z from those three, the Tortoise writes that down too, in the same way. Whatever Achilles offers as a rule, the Tortoise turns into a mere string, and a string cannot make anyone do anything. Each new rule becomes a longer string, and Z never arrives. A rule has to be used; it cannot be replaced by a sentence saying it may be used. At some point, Hofstadter says, you cannot defend your ways of reasoning any further, and trust takes over.

This is the third endless chain the book has shown. In Chapter II's dialogue (page 03) a rule needed a rule. In Chapter VI (page 07) a message needed a second message saying how to read it. Here a proof needs a proof. Each chain ends the same way: not in one more statement, but in something that just acts. A rule gets followed, a brain runs, a reasoner trusts.

So the formal system does not force this page's Tortoise either. Inside the game ∧ cannot be bent, but nothing makes him pick up the rules.

9

Working in the system, and talking about it

Anyone who makes a few derivations starts taking shortcuts. Having derived <P∨~P>, you would not bother to derive <Q∨~Q> step by step. You know the same steps would work with Q in place of P, so you treat the first theorem as a mould for a whole family. A shortcut of this kind is called a derived rule.

Derived rules are safe. But notice where the knowledge that they are safe comes from. It is not a string, and no rule of the system produced it. You reasoned about the system, in ordinary language. Page 02 called this stepping outside the system. Hofstadter now gives the outside view its proper names.

Inside

The system

Working here is what page 02 called the M-mode (mechanical mode: just follow the rules). Its language is strings such as <P∨~P>. Its results are theorems with a small t: strings the rules produce. Its stand-in for a proof is a derivation.

Outside

The metatheory

Thinking here is page 02's I-mode (intelligent mode: think about the rules). Its language is English, the metalanguage ("meta" is Greek for "after" or "beyond"; here it means "about"). Its results are metatheorems: Theorems with a capital T, results proved by ordinary reasoning, about theorems. "Every theorem is true in every world" is one. So is every derived rule. The study of a system from outside is its metatheory.

Why not just allow the shortcuts? Because of what formal systems are for. They exist so that every step is written out inside one rigid framework and anyone, or any machine, can check a derivation without having to think. A derived rule was justified by thinking. Use it, and you have stepped outside the framework you built in order not to step outside.

Then why not make the outside formal too? You could build a second, bigger formal system whose theorems are the derived rules of the first. But soon you would want shortcuts in that system, and a third to justify them. It is the staircase of section 8 again: a theory about a theory about a theory.

Could a system be its own metatheory, so that the staircase folds into a single step? Hofstadter promises that the book will soon meet a system that can talk about itself, and that it comes to do so by accident, without anyone planning it. Even then, he warns, the system does not get outside itself. You, looking on, still see it differently from the way it sees itself. That was the Tortoise's jibe at Achilles: what cannot be seen from inside may be plain from outside.

A mistake that comes from mixing the levels<P∨~P> is a theorem, and it reads "P or not P". So surely either P is a theorem or ~P is? No. Neither is, as the tables in section 7 showed. The ∨ inside the string is a symbol of the system. The "or" in "either this is a theorem or that is" belongs to English, outside. Sliding a symbol from one level to the other is exactly the kind of move that goes wrong.

Proofs and derivations

Earlier pages used proof loosely. Hofstadter now separates two things. A proof is what mathematicians write for each other: ordinary thought in ordinary language. A derivation is its artificial twin inside a formal system.

Proof

Short. Each step "sounds right", though you may not be able to say exactly why. Its hidden complication is the thing it rests on: the whole of human language and thought.

Derivation

Each step is so small that nobody could doubt it. Its complication is sheer size. Ganto's Ax, the optional example in section 6, took two dozen lines to reach one small conclusion, and derivations of real mathematics are far too long to take in.

The Propositional Calculus is a method for building these proof-like structures. Hofstadter is careful to add that its rules are not the rules of human thought.

It is also only one of many versions. Hofstadter notes that his is related to a system invented by the German logician Gerhard Gentzen in the early 1930s. Other versions do have axioms, and often just one rule, detachment. Rules can be traded for axioms and back.

10

What happens when a contradiction gets in

The dialogue began with a contradiction. The chapter ends by feeding one to the system. Take as the premise of a fantasy the string <P∧~P>, "P and not P". With P as "my shell is green" it is the sentence Achilles was trying to extract. To the system it is just another well-formed string.

Line 14 is a theorem: <<P∧~P>⊃Q>. Its semi-interpretation (section 5: read the symbols, leave the atoms alone) is "if P and not P, then Q". But Q never appeared in the premise. It can be any sentence at all.

From a contradiction, anything follows.

If my shell is green and my shell is not green, then politicians lie in cast-iron sinks.

Line 11 uses the contrapositive rule from right to left. Line 10 has the shape <~y⊃~x> with x as ~P and y as Q, so it can be swapped for <x⊃y>, which is <~P⊃Q>.

The table test of section 7 agrees. <x⊃y> is false only in a world where x is true and y false, and there is no world where <P∧~P> is true.

This is why consistency matters so much. If any system built on these rules ever proved both x and ~x, joining would give <x∧~x>, and then this theorem and detachment would give every well-formed string whatsoever. One contradiction and the whole system is worthless. Hofstadter says it spreads like an instant cancer.

People do not work like this

If you found that two of your beliefs contradicted each other, you would not conclude that you now believe everything. (The Tortoise said two opposite things about his shell, and even he did not start believing everything.) You would step back, look for the belief or the habit of reasoning that caused the trouble, and repair it. This is page 02's jumping out of the system: the Propositional Calculus cannot do it, and people do it by habit. Hofstadter goes further and calls contradiction a major source of progress in every field. Mathematics is his example. In past centuries people "proved" that the endless sum 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + … equals 0, and that it equals 1, and that it equals ½. Mathematics did not collapse. The clash led to a better understanding of what an endless sum is.

So here is a gap between the Propositional Calculus and real thinking, and logicians have tried to close it. One attempt Hofstadter mentions is relevant implication, worked out by the logicians Alan Ross Anderson and Nuel Belnap. Its aim is an "if… then" that holds only when the two halves have something to do with each other. The Propositional Calculus makes no such demand. These are all theorems:

<P⊃<Q⊃P>>If P, then: if Q, then P.
<P⊃<Q∨~Q>>If P, then Q or not Q.
<<P∧~P>⊃Q>If P and not P, then Q.
<<P⊃Q>∨<Q⊃P>>If P then Q, or if Q then P.

Take the last one with P as "the shell is green" and Q as "the flax weighs three pounds". Neither has anything to do with the other, yet the theorem reads "if the shell is green then the flax weighs three pounds, or if the flax weighs three pounds then the shell is green". (As section 9 warned, neither half is a theorem on its own.) Relevant implication is designed to rule such theorems out. Other researchers, Hofstadter adds, go further and give up consistency altogether, in order to imitate human reasoning with all its faults.

Where this leaves usFor all its stiffness, the Propositional Calculus does its one job perfectly. Placed inside a larger system that is free of contradictions, it supplies every correct step of "and / or / not / if… then" reasoning, and no incorrect one. So if a larger system ever turns out to have a gap or a flaw, the fault will lie in what was added, not in this part. The next chapter builds such a larger system: it takes these nine rules whole into a system that can talk about numbers.

Recap: the dialogue and chapter in one breath

Words cannot force anyone to reason properly, as the Tortoise shows with "and". Nine rules of shape make ∧, ∨, ~ and ⊃ act like "and", "or", "not" and "if… then", so that every step can be checked without thought and every theorem comes out true whatever its atoms mean. But accepting the rules is still an act of trust.

Dialogue

A feud over "and"

The Tortoise says his shell is green and not green, and Achilles cannot make him join the two. A word does not enforce its own use.

The system

No axioms, one fantasy

Suppose x, reach y, and keep <x⊃y>. Theorems are the patterns true in every world, and a table decides which strings they are.

Outside the system

Trust, levels, limits

No proof that the rules are safe gets beneath your own reasoning. Shortcuts live outside the system. And one contradiction inside it proves everything.

12

Modern view: what we know now

As of November 2026

Hofstadter was careful to say that these rules are not the rules of human thought. Since 1979 that remark has become something researchers can test, in brains and in AI systems. And the chapter's question of how far to trust a system of rules is now daily work for people who check proofs by computer.

AI · looking inside

Where a language model keeps its "or" and "if… then"

In the book: the rules for ∧, ∨ and ⊃ are written out, so anyone can check every step (sections 3, 4 and 6).

Now: a large language model has no written rules, only a huge pile of arithmetic tuned on text. A study published at the NeurIPS 2025 conference (full text) gave three openly available models a few worked examples and then tiny problems such as: A or B implies C; D implies E; A is true, B is false, D is true; what is the truth value of C? The researchers swapped the activity of single parts with their activity on a slightly different problem, to see which ones matter. All three models showed a similar, though not identical, division of labour among four small groups of parts (the largest model was only partly analysed): one finds the rule being asked about, one carries it forward, one handles the facts, one decides. The caveats: each problem needed only one rule to be applied, the models wrote a correct proof only 70% to 86% of the time, and the authors say longer chains still need checking.

Why it matters: nobody gave these models a detachment rule. Something that does its job grew during training, and can be located. But it is a habit that sometimes fails, where the chapter's rule cannot.

AI · reasoning

Models, like people, mix "valid" with "sounds true"

In the book: a theorem holds whatever sentences fill the atoms, koan lines included. The rules look only at shape (section 5).

Now: people find this hard. An argument is valid if its conclusion follows from its starting statements by form alone, whether or not any of them is true. People more readily call an argument valid when its conclusion sounds true. Psychologists call this a content effect, and a 2024 study from Google DeepMind found it in language models too. A 2026 paper looked for the cause inside the models. It built 1,280 arguments from "all", "some" and "no", half ending in something true such as "some dogs are canines", half in something false such as "some dogs are cats". On examples it had not seen, one model, answering at once, got every valid argument with a true conclusion right, but only 61% of the valid ones with a false conclusion. Told to reason step by step first, it reached 93%. Inside, the model's signal for "valid" and its signal for "believable" overlapped, so that pushing on one moved the other. Pulling them apart cut that model's bias to about a fifth. The authors studied mid-sized open models and do not claim to know why the two signals merge.

Why it matters: the system's blindness to content, which makes its theorems sound so empty, is a strength that neither people nor these models have by default.

Neuroscience

A brain study finds logic working outside the language areas

In the book: words cannot enforce their own logic (section 1), and the system's rules are not offered as the rules of human thought (section 9).

Now: if we reasoned in words, the brain areas that handle sentences should work harder when the logic gets harder. A study in the journal PNAS in July 2026 tested this. Twenty-nine adults were scanned, some of them while judging "if… then" arguments. Easy ones used detachment ("if the block is large, then it is not yellow; the block is large; so…"). Hard ones ran backwards, like section 6's contrapositive, from "the block is yellow" to "it is not large". The language areas were busy reading, but no busier for the hard arguments. Other regions were, separate both from the language areas and from the areas that serve demanding tasks in general. The team also tested two men with badly damaged language areas, who did no better than guessing on grammar tests. On puzzles about number lists and shape patterns they did as well as healthy adults. The authors' caveats: two patients is few; the patients could not be given the "if… then" task, which needs reading, so wordless versions are still to be tried; and whether children need language to learn logic is open.

Why it matters: this suggests that the logic is not in the words. In our heads, too, the sentence and the inference seem to run on different machinery.

Mathematics · trust

A proof about the proof checker itself

In the book: any proof that the rules are sound leans on the reasoning it is meant to guarantee. Each is another box around the egg (section 8).

Now: Page 05 told how, in 2026, AI models found programming mistakes in the core of Lean, a proof assistant. The core is the part that accepts or rejects each step. In October 2026 Lean's chief architect announced an additional core, shipped alongside the official one, written in Lean itself and proved consistent: whatever it accepts fits a known mathematical picture of Lean's logic. The AI model Claude wrote and proved it, under "heavy supervision" by a Lean developer. The announcement states the limits. The proof was checked by Lean's official core, the one that had the mistakes, since fixed. It assumes that some mathematics stronger than the usual foundation is itself consistent. And the added core is "not bulletproof", because the software that turns it into a running program may have faults. So it was also translated into another programming language, and users are urged to run several cores and report any disagreement. This is the developers' own account, dated 15 October 2026, and not independently reviewed.

Why it matters: this is Prudence's programme carried out with great care, and it ends where Imprudence said it would: with one more box, and something outside it taken on trust.
