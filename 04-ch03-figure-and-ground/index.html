Sonata for Unaccompanied Achilles + Figure and Ground

GEB

Draw a shape and you have also drawn everything around it. Usually what's left over is just background. Sometimes it is a shape in its own right. This page covers the dialogue Sonata for Unaccompanied Achilles, where half a conversation is left for you to fill in, and Chapter III, where Hofstadter asks the same thing of formal systems: when a system's rules make some strings, are the strings it never makes a pattern too?

Dialogue
Sonata for Unaccompanied Achilles
1

A phone call with one end missing

The story. Achilles is on the telephone with the Tortoise. The page prints only what Achilles says. Wherever the Tortoise speaks, there is a gap.

From Achilles' replies you can still follow the call. The Tortoise has a stiff, twisted neck (the medical word, torticollis, sounds like his name) and a headache. He got the stiff neck from staring too long, head tilted, at a print by M. C. Escher in which creatures fit together so tightly that the gaps between the dark ones are themselves light ones. The talk turns to Bach's sonatas for a violin playing alone, and how one violin can be enough. Then they trade two word puzzles: one asks for a word with the letters A, D, A, C in a row inside it; the other for a word that both begins and ends with the letters HE. Achilles tries hard, gets one of them backwards, and they arrange to meet.

The answer to the two word puzzlesTry them first: a word containing ADAC, and a word that starts and ends with HE.One word solves both.

The same word answers both: HEADACHE. It contains ADAC (headache), and it starts and ends with HE. The answer was in the story all along: the Tortoise has one. Achilles never notices. His own attempt finds the four letters running backwards, C-A-D-A, inside "abracadabra": he has looked at the puzzle in reverse.

One puzzle describes the middle of the word and the other describes its two ends. Each is what the other leaves out.

The form: a sonata for one instrument

A sonata is a piece of music in several movements (separate parts, usually fast, slow, fast) for one or two instruments. A violin sonata normally comes with a keyboard playing along: the accompaniment. Around 1720 Bach wrote three sonatas and three partitas (sets of dances) for a violin with nothing playing along at all. Musicians call such a piece unaccompanied.

A violin mostly plays one note at a time, so these pieces are mostly a single line of notes. Yet listeners hear a tune and a bass under it. Section 2 lets you hear how.

The dialogue copies this. It is a piece for two voices with only one written down.

The hidden trickYou never read a word from the Tortoise, and you know roughly what he said anyway. Achilles' printed lines are a shape; the Tortoise's lines are the space around that shape; and the space turns out to be a second, readable conversation. The title says so: Achilles is "unaccompanied" on the page only.
2

One line, two voices

Here is how one violin can sound like two. The line below is a single string of notes, one after another, never two at once. But it keeps hopping between a high region and a low region. The ear groups the high notes into one tune and the low notes into another, and fills in each tune across the hops.

high noteslow notes

An original sketch in the style of Bach's solo violin music, not his notes, played on a synthesised plucked sound rather than a violin. On their own, the high and low notes are each held longer, to show the tune the ear fills in.

Play the single line, then each half. Neither half is written as a separate part. Each is what remains when you take the other away, and each makes sense by itself.

Painters have words for this. The thing a picture shows is its figure. Everything around it is the ground, short for background. In the single line above you can treat the high notes as figure and the low notes as ground, or the other way round.

The idea the dialogue sets upWhen you make one thing, you also make its leftover. The Tortoise's half of the call, the bass under the violin's tune, the gaps between Escher's animals: each leftover here was a real thing with its own shape. The chapter now asks whether that is always so. Its test case is a formal system, the rule-following symbol game of page 02. The rules produce some rows of symbols and never produce others. Is everything they never produce also something that rules could produce?
Chapter III
Figure and Ground
3

A system that multiplies

The chapter begins with prime numbers: whole numbers bigger than 1 that no smaller number except 1 divides evenly, such as 2, 3, 5 and 7. Hofstadter's question: can a formal system, which only shuffles symbols, produce the primes and nothing else? He builds up to it in three steps: first multiplication, then the numbers that are not prime, then the primes themselves.

Words from earlier pages. A string is a row of symbols. An axiom is a string you are given to start with. A rule of production makes a new string from ones you already have. A theorem is any string you can reach from the axioms by the rules, and the record of how you reached it is its derivation. A theorem here is only a string the rules allow. It need not mean anything.

Chapter II had a system built from the letters p, q and hyphens, whose theorems looked like --p---q-----: two hyphens, three hyphens, five hyphens. Read p as "plus" and q as "equals", count the hyphens, and every theorem is a correct addition. The system knows nothing about adding. Its rules only shuffle symbols, and the sums come out right all the same.

The tq-system

The new system does the same for multiplication. Its symbols are t, q and the hyphen. A hyphen-string is a row of one or more hyphens and nothing else. Below, x, y and z each stand for some hyphen-string.

Axioms
x t - q x

Any hyphen-string, then t, one hyphen, q, and the same hyphen-string again.

Example: ---t-q--- (3 times 1 equals 3)
Rule
x t y q z → x t y- q zx

From a theorem, make a new one: add one hyphen in the middle group, and add a copy of the first group to the last group.

Example: ---t-q--- → ---t--q------ (3 times 2 equals 6)

There are endlessly many axioms, one for each hyphen-string. A pattern like this, which says what shape every axiom has, is called an axiom schema.

Read t as "times" and q as "equals" and every theorem is a true multiplication. The rule is how you multiply by counting: "3 times 2" is "3 times 1" with one more 3 added on. Once again the system does not multiply. It copies hyphens.

4

Capturing the composite numbers

A composite number is a whole number you get by multiplying two smaller numbers, each at least 2. So 6 is composite (2 times 3), 9 is composite (3 times 3), and 7 is not. (Without "each at least 2" every number would count, since any number is 1 times itself.) Every whole number bigger than 1 is either composite or prime, never both.

With multiplication in hand, composites take one more rule. The new symbol C will mark them.

Rule of the C-system
x- t y- q z → C z

If a tq-theorem has at least two hyphens in its first group and at least two in its second, you may write C followed by its last group.

Example: ---t--q------ → C------ (6 is composite)

Writing x- is how a rule says "at least two hyphens" using shapes alone: some hyphen-string, plus one more hyphen. Try it. Pick two numbers and the page writes out the derivation, then marks the result on the number strip.

Derivation
    Numbers 1 to 40: those with a C-theorem so far

    The system makes C followed by n hyphens exactly when n is composite. A property of numbers, "being composite", has been turned into a property of strings, "being a theorem of the C-system".

    Now look at the squares the system never fills. Apart from 1, they are the primes. So the primes are sitting right there, as the holes in the pattern. Surely that is enough to capture them?

    5

    The rule that isn't a rule

    The obvious next step is a rule that reads off the holes, with a new symbol P for "prime":

    Proposed rule (not allowed)
    C x is not a theorem → P x

    If C followed by some hyphen-string is not a theorem, write P followed by that hyphen-string.

    Hofstadter rejects it, and the reason is the heart of the chapter. Every rule so far could be carried out by a clerk who understands nothing: look at a string in front of you, check its shape, copy pieces of it. He calls such steps typographical operations (typography is the craft of arranging printed symbols). His list is short: reading and recognising symbols, writing them down, copying them, erasing them, checking whether two symbols are the same, and keeping a list of the theorems made so far.

    "Is not a theorem" is not on the list. To use the proposed rule, the clerk would need to know that C------- will never turn up. No string in front of him shows that. He could keep producing C-theorems and watching, but page 02 showed where that leads: producing theorems one after another tells you "yes" when a string appears and never tells you "no". Knowing that 7 hyphens will never follow a C takes reasoning about the whole system from outside it. A rule is only allowed to work inside.

    Holes are not automatically a shapeThe C-system draws the composites. The primes are what it leaves blank. But "what the rules leave blank" is not something rules can point at. If the primes are to be theorems, some system has to produce them directly, by rules that only ever say "this follows from that". Before building one, Hofstadter stops to ask how often a leftover can be produced directly at all.
    6

    Figure and ground in pictures and music

    Section 2 gave the two words: the figure is what a picture shows, the ground is what surrounds it. Hofstadter also calls them positive space and negative space. He picks out a special kind of figure. Tap the parts of each picture below to see the difference.

    One bird on a sheet of paper

    A sheet filled with birds

    Original drawings. Tap a bird, or the space around one.

    On the left, the ground is whatever the bird happened to leave: a sheet with a bird-shaped hole. Nobody would draw that on purpose. Hofstadter calls any figure you can simply draw cursively drawable: you draw it as you would write a word in cursive handwriting, attending only to the pen line, and the ground is whatever results.

    On the right, the ground of the dark birds is the light birds, and the ground of the light birds is the dark ones. Whichever you call the figure, the leftover is a figure too: its ground could have been drawn on purpose as well. Hofstadter calls such a figure recursive. He borrows the word from the mathematics of the next section, and here it means only this: the ground is also a figure. So recursive figures are the special ones among the cursively drawable. The left-hand bird is cursively drawable and not recursive.

    The chapter's examples are Escher's prints of interlocking creatures, such as Mosaic II, the print Achilles hears about on the phone, and a drawing by Scott Kim in which the word FIGURE is written in black letters so that the white gaps between them spell FIGURE as well. Escher's work is still under copyright, so it is not reproduced here; you can see his tilings at the official M. C. Escher gallery.

    In music, the usual figure is the melody and the usual ground is the accompaniment, which you are not meant to notice much. Hofstadter points out that Bach rarely leaves it at that. His lower parts are tunes worth following for their own sake, and in the solo violin pieces of section 2 a single line carries both. That is a recursive figure made of sound.

    The lesson to carry forward: some figures have a ground that is a figure, and some do not. It depends on the figure. Hofstadter admits that "a recognisable shape" is a loose idea for drawings. For formal systems it can be made exact.

    7

    Theorems are a figure. Is their ground one too?

    Back to formal systems. Take every string that can be written with a system's symbols. The theorems are some of them: that is the figure the rules draw. All the other strings, the non-theorems, are the ground. Section 5 showed that you cannot get at this ground by saying "whatever isn't a theorem". The remaining hope is that the ground has rules of its own: a second formal system whose theorems are exactly the first system's non-theorems.

    Two terms make the question precise. Both describe a set of strings or of numbers.

    1Recursively enumerable: a figure

    Think of a formal system as a machine that prints its theorems one after another, forever. A set is recursively enumerable if some formal system prints exactly its members. "Enumerable" means listable: every member turns up on the list sooner or later, and nothing else does. The composites are recursively enumerable, because the C-system lists them. This is the cursively drawable figure: something rules can trace directly.

    2Recursive: figure and ground both

    A set is recursive if it is recursively enumerable and everything outside it is recursively enumerable too. One machine lists the members; a second machine lists the non-members. This is the recursive figure: one whose ground is also a figure.

    3Two lists make a test that always finishes

    Page 02 introduced a decision procedure: a test that is guaranteed to finish and say yes or no. It also showed that one machine listing theorems is not such a test. If your string appears, you have a "yes". If it has not appeared yet, you learn nothing, however long you wait.

    A second machine fixes this. Run both side by side, a step of one and then a step of the other. Your string is either a member or a non-member, so it must turn up on one of the two lists. Wait until it does, and see which list it is on. The "no" you could never get from the first machine arrives as a "yes" from the second.

    It works the other way round as well. If you have a decision procedure, you can build the second machine from it: go through every possible string in turn, test each, and print the ones that get a "no". So "the ground is a figure" and "there is a decision procedure" are the same thing.

    4The surprise

    It would be natural to guess that a second machine can always be built: that whenever rules can list a set, some other rules can list what is left out. Hofstadter states that the guess is false. He does not prove it here; the proof needs ideas from much later in the book.

    There are formal systems whose non-theorems are not the theorems of any formal system.

    The same fact in the new terms: some sets are recursively enumerable but not recursive. And in terms of tests: some formal systems have no decision procedure. Their theorems can be listed, and no mechanical method can tell you, for every string, that it will never appear.

    The MIU-system of page 02 is not one of these. It looked as if it might be, and then section 8 of that page found a test for it. The systems that truly have none are richer, and the book reaches them later. The Introduction told how Alan Turing and Alonzo Church found the first such cases in 1936.

    The Introduction also said that Gödel found true statements about numbers that their formal system can never prove. This chapter gives a first picture of how that can happen. The rules trace out the theorems as a pen traces a line, and what they leave behind can have a shape that no rules trace. Hofstadter calls this fact as deep as Gödel's theorem itself. The next page starts to ask what separates "true" from "is a theorem".

    8

    A number puzzle about figure and ground

    Hofstadter then sets a puzzle to make the idea concrete with numbers. Here is a list of numbers that goes on forever. What is the rule, and what comes next?

    numbers in the listgaps between them

    Hofstadter leaves this one for the reader to work out. The list and its gaps are figure and ground for the whole numbers: each is exactly what the other leaves out. And here both are produced by one simple rule, so both are figures. The set of numbers in the list is recursive.

    9

    Primes as a figure, built from the positive side

    Now the primes. They are the ground of the composites, and section 5 showed they cannot be reached as "the non-composites". Hofstadter shows they can be produced directly. The trick is to replace the forbidden question, "is this string not a theorem?", with theorems that state a negative fact outright.

    Being prime means: 2 does not divide it, 3 does not divide it, and so on up to the number just below it. So the system first makes theorems that say "does not divide", then collects them.

    1"Does not divide" as a theorem

    The new symbol is DND. The string --DND--- is to be read "2 does not divide 3".

    Axioms
    xy DND x

    A longer hyphen-string, DND, then a shorter one.

    Example: ---DND-- (3 does not divide 2, since 3 is bigger)
    Rule
    x DND y → x DND xy

    Add a copy of the left group to the right group.

    Example: ---DND-- → ---DND----- (if 3 does not divide 2, it does not divide 2 + 3)
    2Collecting the checks

    The symbol DF stands for "divisor-free up to". The string -----DF--- is to be read "nothing from 2 up to 3 divides 5".

    Rule: start
    -- DND z → z DF --

    If 2 does not divide z, then z is divisor-free up to 2.

    Rule: one more
    z DF x and x- DND z → z DF x-

    If z is divisor-free up to x, and the next number after x does not divide z either, extend the check by one.

    Rule: prime
    z- DF z → P z-

    If a number is divisor-free all the way up to the number just below it, write P and that number.

    One extra axiom
    P --

    2 is prime. It needs its own axiom because there is nothing between 2 and itself to check.

    Every rule here is a typographical operation: compare shapes, copy hyphens. None asks what is not a theorem. Yet P followed by n hyphens is a theorem exactly when n is prime.

    3Run both systems side by side

    Here are section 7's two machines. Pick a number and run them. Each prints its theorems in order of length, one per step. To save space, C·6 stands for C followed by 6 hyphens.

    C-system prints
    P-system prints
    How the system made that theorem

      For every number from 2 up, exactly one of the two machines prints it, so the wait always ends. Neither machine ever says "no". Each "no" is read off from the other machine's "yes". The composites are a figure, their ground (leaving aside 1) is the primes, and the primes are a figure too. So the set of primes is recursive, and "is this number prime?" has a decision procedure.

      Why it worked here. Step 3 of section 7 said a ground can be listed exactly when there is a decision procedure. Composites have an easy one. To test a number, you only need to try multiplying the smaller numbers, and there are only so many of those. The rules of the tq-system and the C-system only ever make strings longer, so nothing short can appear late. The P-system is that finite check, written out as rules. Trouble can come with systems whose rules both lengthen and shorten strings, as the MIU rules did: there a short string might appear after a long detour, so waiting alone never rules it out. (For MIU a different trick settled the matter. Sometimes nothing does.)

      Notice also what it cost. The ground did not come free with the figure. Someone had to find new rules, with new symbols, that state the negative facts positively. For primes that could be done. Section 7's claim is that for some systems it cannot.

      10

      Recap: the dialogue and chapter in one breath

      Whatever you make leaves a leftover. Sometimes the leftover is a shape of its own, like the Tortoise's half of the call or the primes among the numbers. Sometimes it is not, and then no rules can produce it.

      Dialogue

      One line, two voices

      Only Achilles' half of a phone call is printed, and the Tortoise's half can be read from the gaps, as a solo violin line carries a tune and a bass at once.

      Figure and ground

      Two kinds of figure

      A cursively drawable figure leaves an accidental ground. A recursive figure, like interlocking birds, has a ground that is a figure too.

      Formal systems

      Theorems and non-theorems

      Rules can produce composites, and by different rules, primes. But "not a theorem" is never a rule, and some systems' non-theorems cannot be produced by any rules at all.
