Sonata for Unaccompanied Achilles + Figure and Ground

GEB

Draw a shape and you have also drawn everything around it. Usually what's left over is just background. Sometimes it is a shape in its own right. This page covers the dialogue Sonata for Unaccompanied Achilles, where half a conversation is left for you to fill in, and Chapter III, where Hofstadter asks the same thing of games played with symbols and rules: when the rules produce some rows of symbols, are the rows they never produce a pattern too?

Dialogue
Sonata for Unaccompanied Achilles
1

A phone call with one end missing

The story. Achilles and the Tortoise are the two characters who talk their way through the book's dialogues. Here Achilles is on the telephone with the Tortoise, and the book prints only what Achilles says. Wherever the Tortoise speaks, there is a gap.

From Achilles' replies you can still follow the call. The Tortoise has a stiff, twisted neck (the medical word, torticollis, sounds like his name) and a headache. It seems he held his head in one position too long, staring at a crowd of strange creatures (it sounds like the Escher print that comes up next). They talk about Bach's sonatas for a violin playing alone, and whether such music needs anything playing along with it or leaves the listener to imagine the rest.

Then come two word puzzles. The Tortoise asks for a word with the letters A, D, A, C in a row inside it. Achilles answers with a puzzle of his own: a word that both begins and ends with the letters HE. The call hints that the two puzzles are related as "figure" and "ground" (the thing a picture shows, and its background). To explain those words the Tortoise has Achilles look at a print by the Dutch artist M. C. Escher, in which dark creatures and light creatures fit together so tightly that each kind is the space between the other. Achilles tries hard, gets the first puzzle backwards, and they arrange to meet.

The answer to the two word puzzlesTry them first: a word containing ADAC, and a word that starts and ends with HE.One word solves both.

The same word answers both: HEADACHE. It contains ADAC (headache), and it starts and ends with HE. The answer was in the story all along: the Tortoise has one. Achilles never notices. His own attempt finds the four letters running backwards, C-A-D-A, inside "abracadabra": he has looked at the puzzle in reverse.

One puzzle describes the middle of the word and the other describes its two ends. Each is what the other leaves out.

The form: a sonata for one instrument

A sonata is a piece of music in several movements (separate parts) for one or two instruments. A violin sonata normally comes with a keyboard playing along: the accompaniment. Around 1720 Bach wrote six pieces, three of them called sonatas, for a violin with nothing playing along at all. Musicians call such a piece unaccompanied.

A violin mostly plays one note at a time, so these pieces are mostly a single line of notes. Yet listeners hear a tune and a second, lower line under it. Section 2 lets you hear how.

The dialogue copies this. It is a piece for two speakers with only one written down.

The hidden trickYou never read a word from the Tortoise, and you know roughly what he said anyway. Achilles' printed lines are a shape; the Tortoise's lines are the space around that shape; and the space turns out to be a second, readable conversation. The title says so: Achilles is "unaccompanied" on the page only.
2

One line, two voices

Here is how one violin can sound like two. The line below is a single run of notes, one after another, never two started at once. But it keeps hopping between a high region and a low region. The ear groups the high notes into one tune and the low notes into another, and fills in each tune across the hops.

high noteslow notes

An original sketch in the style of Bach's solo violin music, not his notes, played on a synthesised plucked sound rather than a violin. Each note rings on while the next two begin, as a plucked string does.

Play the single line, then each half. Neither half is written as a separate part. Each is what remains when you take the other away, and each makes sense by itself.

Painters have words for this. The thing a picture shows is its figure. Everything around it is the ground, short for background. In the single line above you can treat the high notes as figure and the low notes as ground, or the other way round.

The idea the dialogue sets upWhen you make one thing, you also make its leftover. The Tortoise's half of the call, the low line under the violin's tune, the gaps between Escher's animals: each leftover here was a real thing with its own shape. The chapter now asks whether that is always so. Its test case is a formal system, the rule-following symbol game of page 02. The rules produce some rows of symbols and never produce others. Is everything they never produce also something that some other set of rules could produce?
Chapter III
Figure and Ground
3

A system that multiplies

The example Hofstadter tries the question on is the prime numbers: whole numbers bigger than 1 that no smaller number except 1 divides evenly, such as 2, 3, 5 and 7. Hofstadter's question: can a formal system, which only shuffles symbols, produce the primes and nothing else? The primes will turn out to be a leftover: what remains when rules have produced all the other numbers. He builds up to it in three steps: first multiplication, then the numbers that are not prime, then the primes themselves.

Words from earlier pages. A string is a row of symbols. An axiom is a string you are given to start with. A rule of production makes a new string from ones you already have. A theorem is any string you can reach from the axioms by the rules, and the record of how you reached it is its derivation. A theorem here is only a string the rules allow. It need not mean anything.

Chapter II of the book has a system built from the letters p, q and hyphens, whose theorems look like --p---q-----: two hyphens, three hyphens, five hyphens. Read p as "plus" and q as "equals", count the hyphens, and every theorem is a correct addition. The system knows nothing about adding. Its rules only shuffle symbols, and the sums come out right all the same.

The tq-system

The new system does the same for multiplication. Its symbols are t, q and the hyphen. A hyphen-string is a row of one or more hyphens and nothing else. Below, x, y and z each stand for some hyphen-string. The patterns are printed with spaces to make them easier to read; the strings themselves have none.

Axioms
x t - q x

Any hyphen-string, then t, one hyphen, q, and the same hyphen-string again.

Example: ---t-q--- (3 times 1 equals 3)
Rule
x t y q z → x t y- q zx

From a theorem, make a new one: add one hyphen in the middle group, and add a copy of the first group to the last group.

Example: ---t-q--- → ---t--q------ (3 times 2 equals 6)

There are endlessly many axioms, one for each hyphen-string.

Read t as "times" and q as "equals" and every theorem is a true multiplication. The rule is how you multiply by counting: "3 times 2" is "3 times 1" with one more 3 added on. Once again the system does not multiply. It copies hyphens.

4

A system for the composite numbers

A composite number is a whole number you get by multiplying two smaller numbers, each at least 2. So 6 is composite (2 times 3), 9 is composite (3 times 3), and 7 is not. Every whole number bigger than 1 is either composite or prime, never both.

With multiplication in hand, composites take one more rule. The new symbol C will mark them. In the rule, x- is how to say "at least two hyphens" using shapes alone: some hyphen-string, plus one more hyphen.

Rule of the C-system
x- t y- q z → C z

If a tq-theorem has at least two hyphens in its first group and at least two in its second, you may write C followed by its last group.

Example: ---t--q------ → C------ (6 is composite)

The tq-system together with this rule is the C-system. Try it. Pick two numbers (with a product up to 40) and the demo writes out the derivation, then marks the result on the number strip.

Derivation
    Numbers 1 to 40: those with a C-theorem so far

    The system makes C followed by n hyphens exactly when n is composite. A property of numbers, "being composite", has been turned into a property of strings, "being a theorem of the C-system".

    Press "Make every C-theorem up to 40" and look at the squares that stay empty. Apart from 1, they are the primes. So the primes are sitting right there, as the holes in the pattern. Surely that is enough to pin them down?

    5

    The rule that isn't a rule

    The obvious next step is a rule that reads off the holes, with a new symbol P for "prime":

    Proposed rule (not allowed)
    C x is not a theorem → P x

    If C followed by some hyphen-string is not a theorem, write P followed by that hyphen-string.

    Hofstadter rejects it, and the reason is the heart of the chapter. Every rule so far could be carried out by a clerk who understands nothing: look at a string in front of you, check its shape, copy pieces of it. He calls such steps typographical operations (typography is the craft of arranging printed symbols). His list is short: reading and recognising symbols, writing them down, copying them, erasing them, checking whether two symbols are the same, and keeping a list of the theorems made so far.

    "Is not a theorem" is not on the list. To use the proposed rule, the clerk would need to know that C------- will never turn up. No string in front of him shows that. He could keep producing C-theorems and watching, but page 02 showed where that leads: producing theorems one after another tells you "yes" when a string appears and never tells you "no". Knowing that 7 hyphens will never follow a C takes reasoning about the whole system from outside it. A rule is only allowed to work inside.

    You can see that 7 will never appear: the only products of two numbers from 2 up that stay below 8 are 2 times 2 = 4 and 2 times 3 = 6, and every other one overshoots. That is exactly the outside reasoning the clerk is not allowed. Page 02 had names for the two ways of working: the clerk is in M-mode, applying rules inside the system, and you have just used I-mode, stepping outside to see what the rules can never do. Section 9 will turn it into rules he can follow.

    Holes are not automatically a shapeThe C-system draws the composites. The primes are what it leaves blank. But "what the rules leave blank" is not something rules can point at. If the primes are to be theorems, some system has to produce them directly, by rules that only ever say "this follows from that". Before building one, Hofstadter stops to ask how often a leftover can be produced directly at all.
    6

    Figure and ground in pictures and music

    Section 2 gave the two words: the figure is what a picture shows, the ground is what surrounds it. Before asking about the leftovers of formal systems, Hofstadter asks about the leftovers of drawings. Among figures he picks out a special kind.

    One bird on a sheet of paper

    A sheet filled with birds

    Original drawings. The bird is a simple tile: a wing on top, a head and beak at the right.

    On the left, the ground is the rest of the sheet. Its only outline is the one it borrows from the bird, and it is not a picture of anything. Hofstadter calls a figure cursively drawable when its ground is like this, a by-product of drawing the figure: you draw it as you would write a word in cursive (joined-up) handwriting, attending only to the pen line, and the ground is whatever results.

    On the right, the ground of the dark birds is the light birds, and the ground of the light birds is the dark ones. Whichever you call the figure, the leftover is a figure too: its ground could have been drawn on purpose as well. Hofstadter calls such a figure recursive. The word comes from the mathematics of the next section, and he offers a way to remember it: figure and ground can both be drawn cursively, so the picture is drawable twice over, "re-cursive". The left-hand bird is cursively drawable and not recursive.

    The chapter's examples are an Escher tiling of interlocking birds and a drawing by Scott Kim in which the word FIGURE is written in black letters so that the white gaps between them spell FIGURE as well. The print in the dialogue is Escher's Mosaic II. Escher's work is still under copyright, so it is not reproduced here; you can see his tilings at the official M. C. Escher gallery.

    In music, the usual figure is the melody and the usual ground is the accompaniment, which you are not meant to notice much. Hofstadter points out that Bach rarely leaves it at that. His lower parts are tunes worth following for their own sake, and in the solo violin pieces of section 2 a single line carries both, one voice on the beats and the other between them. That is a recursive figure made of sound.

    The lesson to carry forward: some figures have a ground that is a figure, and some do not. Hofstadter admits that what counts as a figure is a loose idea for drawings. For formal systems it can be made exact.

    7

    Theorems are a figure. Is their ground one too?

    Back to formal systems. Take every string that can be written with a system's symbols. The theorems are some of them: that is the figure the rules draw. All the other strings, the non-theorems, are the ground. Section 5 showed that you cannot get at this ground by saying "whatever isn't a theorem". The remaining hope is that the ground has rules of its own: a second formal system whose theorems are exactly the first system's non-theorems.

    Two terms make the question precise. Both describe a set of strings or of numbers.

    1Recursively enumerable: a figure

    Think of a formal system as a machine that prints its theorems one after another, forever. A set is recursively enumerable if some formal system prints exactly its members. "Enumerable" means listable: every member turns up on the list sooner or later, and nothing else does. The composites are recursively enumerable, because the C-system lists them (count only its theorems that start with C, and read the hyphens as a number). This is the cursively drawable figure: something rules can trace directly.

    2Recursive: figure and ground both

    A set is recursive if it is recursively enumerable and everything outside it is recursively enumerable too. One machine lists the members; a second machine lists the non-members. This is the recursive figure: one whose ground is also a figure. (The two names are easy to mix up. "Recursively enumerable" is the weaker property; "recursive", drawable twice over, is the stronger.)

    3Two lists make a test that always finishes

    Page 02 introduced a decision procedure: a test that is guaranteed to finish and say yes or no. It also showed that one machine listing theorems is not such a test. If your string appears, you have a "yes". If it has not appeared yet, you learn nothing, however long you wait.

    A second machine fixes this. Run both side by side, a step of one and then a step of the other. Your string is either a member or a non-member, so it must turn up on one of the two lists. Wait until it does, and see which list it is on. The "no" you could never get from the first machine arrives as a "yes" from the second.

    For example: is 7 composite? The C-system never says no. But if some second system listed the numbers that are not composite, 7 would turn up on its list, and that would settle it. Section 9 builds that second system.

    It works the other way round as well. If you have a decision procedure, you can build the second machine from it: go through every possible string in turn, test each, and print the ones that get a "no". So "the ground is a figure" and "there is a decision procedure" are the same thing.

    4The surprise

    It would be natural to guess that a second machine can always be built: that whenever rules can list a set, some other rules can list what is left out. Hofstadter states that the guess is false. He does not prove it here; the proof needs ideas from much later in the book.

    There are formal systems whose non-theorems are not the theorems of any formal system.

    The same fact in the new terms: some sets are recursively enumerable but not recursive. And in terms of tests: some formal systems have no decision procedure. Their theorems can be listed, and no mechanical method can pick out every string that will never appear.

    Recursive. A second system produces exactly the rest. Section 9 shows this for the composites and the primes.

    Recursively enumerable, not recursive. The ground exists, and no rules trace it.

    The MIU-system of page 02 (strings of the letters M, I and U) is not one of these: it looked as if it might be, and then a test was found for it. The systems that truly have no test are richer, and the book reaches them later.

    Hofstadter calls this fact as deep as Gödel's theorem, the result from the Introduction that some true statements about numbers can never be proved in their formal system. The two are closely linked: a system with no decision procedure is one that leaves some questions unanswered.

    How this connects to Gödel's theoremAn outline, for the curious. The chapter only states the connection; later pages make it exact.
    1. Suppose a system's strings are read as statements about numbers, and every statement has an opposite, as "7 is prime" has "7 is not prime".
    2. Suppose the system never produces both a statement and its opposite.
    3. If it always produced one of the two, you would have a decision procedure: list theorems until the statement or its opposite turns up.
    4. So if such a system has no decision procedure, there is a statement where neither it nor its opposite is ever a theorem.
    5. One of the two is true. That is a true statement the system cannot prove.

    A number puzzle about figure and ground

    Hofstadter then sets a puzzle to make the idea concrete with numbers. Here is a list of numbers that goes on forever. What is the rule, and what comes next? He leaves it for the reader to work out.

    numbers in the listgaps between them

    The list and its gaps are figure and ground for the whole numbers: each is exactly what the other leaves out. And here both are produced by one simple rule, so both are figures. The set of numbers in the list is recursive.

    9

    Primes as a figure, built from the positive side

    Now the primes. They are the ground of the composites, and section 5 showed they cannot be reached as "the non-composites". Hofstadter shows they can be produced directly. The trick is to replace the forbidden question, "is this string not a theorem?", with theorems that state a negative fact outright.

    Being prime means: 2 does not divide it, 3 does not divide it, and so on up to the number just below it. So the system first makes theorems that say "does not divide", then collects them.

    1"Does not divide" as a theorem

    The new symbol is DND. The string --DND--- is to be read "2 does not divide 3".

    Axioms
    xy DND x

    Here xy means x with y stuck on its end. So: a longer hyphen-string, DND, then a shorter one. A bigger number never divides a smaller one.

    Example: ---DND-- (3 does not divide 2, since 3 is bigger)
    Rule
    x DND y → x DND xy

    Add a copy of the left group to the right group.

    Example: ---DND-- → ---DND----- (if 3 does not go evenly into 2, it does not go evenly into 2 + 3 either). In the same way --DND- gives --DND---.
    2Collecting the checks

    The symbol DF stands for "divisor-free up to". A divisor of a number is a number that divides it evenly. The string -----DF--- is to be read "nothing from 2 up to 3 divides 5".

    Rule: start
    -- DND z → z DF --

    If 2 does not divide z, then z is divisor-free up to 2.

    Example: --DND----- → -----DF--
    Rule: one more
    z DF x and x- DND z → z DF x-

    If z is divisor-free up to x, and the next number after x does not divide z either, extend the check by one.

    Example: -----DF-- and ---DND----- → -----DF---
    Rule: prime
    z- DF z → P z-

    If a number is divisor-free all the way up to the number just below it, write P and that number.

    Example: -----DF---- → P----- (5 is prime)
    One extra axiom
    P --

    2 is prime. It needs its own axiom because there is nothing between 2 and itself to check.

    Call all of this together the P-system. Every rule in it is a typographical operation: compare shapes, copy hyphens. None asks what is not a theorem. Yet P followed by n hyphens is a theorem exactly when n is prime.

    3Run both systems side by side

    Here are section 7's two machines. Pick a number and run them. The picture is simplified: it shows only each system's C and P theorems, smallest first, one per step. To save space, C·6 stands for C followed by 6 hyphens.

    C-system prints
    P-system prints
    How the system made that theorem

      For every number from 2 up, exactly one of the two machines prints it, so the wait always ends. Neither machine ever says "no". Each "no" is read off from the other machine's "yes". The composites are a figure, their ground (leaving aside 1) is the primes, and the primes are a figure too. So the set of primes is recursive, and "is this number prime?" has a decision procedure.

      It worked here because composites already have an easy test: try every multiplication of smaller numbers. The P-system is that test written out as rules.

      Why waiting works for composites and not in generalAnd why this does not undo section 5.
      1. In the C-system, the number after C is never smaller than the groups it was multiplied from, and the tq rule only makes groups bigger.
      2. So once either group has passed 7, C------- can never appear. Only a few multiplications need trying, and then you know.
      3. That is still reasoning about the system from outside, which section 5 said a rule may not do. The P-system is the same reasoning turned into rules that work inside.
      4. Systems whose rules both lengthen and shorten strings give no such limit. The MIU-system of page 02 was one: a short string might appear after a long detour through longer ones, so waiting never ruled anything out. Its test came from a separate insight.

      Notice also what it cost. The ground did not come free with the figure. Someone had to find new rules, with new symbols, that state the negative facts positively. For primes that could be done. Section 7's claim is that for some systems it cannot.

      10

      Recap: the dialogue and chapter in one breath

      Whatever you make leaves a leftover. Sometimes the leftover is a shape of its own, like the Tortoise's half of the call or the primes among the numbers. Sometimes it is not, and then no rules can produce it.

      Dialogue

      One line, two voices

      Only Achilles' half of a phone call is printed, and the Tortoise's half can be read from the gaps, as a solo violin line carries a tune and a bass at once.

      Figure and ground

      Two kinds of figure

      A cursively drawable figure is drawn directly, and its ground is whatever is left. A recursive figure, like interlocking birds, has a ground that is a figure too.

      Formal systems

      Theorems and non-theorems

      Rules can produce composites, and by different rules, primes. But "not a theorem" is never a rule, and some systems' non-theorems cannot be produced by any rules at all. Those are the systems with no decision procedure.

      11

      Modern view: what we know now

      As of November 2026

      This page asked when a leftover is a shape of its own: the missing half of a phone call, the space around a bird, the strings a system never makes. Each version of that question is now studied directly, in AI models, in the brain and in mathematics.

      AI · dialogue

      Machines asked to write the Tortoise's half

      In the book: only Achilles' side of the call is printed, and the reader rebuilds the Tortoise's side from the gaps (section 1).

      Now: this is a named research task. A 2026 conference paper calls it the "one-sided conversation problem": a large language model (the kind of AI system behind chatbots such as ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude) is shown one speaker's lines and must write the other's. It matters in practice, because call centres and smart glasses often record only one person. In a small test, 16 human judges compared the real reply with one written by a large model, without being told which was which. For 25 conversations about bookings, a clear majority chose the model's reply as the better fit in 56% of them and the real reply in 12%; the rest were ties or split votes. The authors' caveat: a reply that fits is not the reply that was given. Scores for matching the real meaning stayed low, and the models sometimes invented details.

      Why it matters: half a conversation does carry a great deal of the other half, enough for a machine to write something people accept. But the ground is only fully readable when the figure was made that way. Hofstadter wrote Achilles' lines so that the Tortoise's could be recovered; ordinary speakers don't.

      AI · neuroscience

      Which side owns the edge?

      In the book: an outline is shared by a figure and its ground, and in Escher's tilings either side can be taken as the figure (section 6).

      Now: the brain settles this edge by edge. In two seeing areas of a monkey's brain, 30 to 50% of the nerve cells respond differently to the very same edge depending on which side of it is the object. Researchers call this border ownership. The same thing appears in machines nobody taught about objects. A piece of software built from many simple units, each one an artificial neuron loosely modelled on a nerve cell, was trained only to predict the next frame of a video. Some of its units came to signal border ownership too, and switching those off hurt its predictions for videos of moving objects more than switching off other units did (2025).

      Why it matters: "figure" is not in the picture. Something has to decide it, and in brains and in machines we can now point to the parts that do. An Escher tiling is the case built so that this decision has two good answers.

      AI · negation

      A "not" added from outside doesn't stick

      In the book: "is not a theorem" can't be a rule. The primes had to be reached by theorems that state a negative fact outright, like "2 does not divide 3" (sections 5 and 9).

      Now: AI models have a matching weak spot. A 2026 study (not yet peer-reviewed; latest version November 2026) gave language models extra training on made-up news stories, for instance that the singer Ed Sheeran won the Olympic 100 metres, with warnings all around saying the story is false. Afterwards the models answered as if the story were true. In the main model tested, averaged over six invented claims, belief rose from 2.5% of answers to 88.6%, almost the 92.4% reached with no warnings at all. When each sentence carried its own "not" ("Ed Sheeran did not win"), the models mostly learned it correctly (tested on two of the claims). The caveats: this is about training on specially written documents, and the same models do spot the falsehood when the documents are simply shown to them in a conversation.

      Why it matters: this is an echo of the chapter, not the same result. A machine that learns from examples picks up what is put in front of it; "and this isn't so", said from outside, is easy to lose. What worked was what Hofstadter did for the primes: write the negative fact as a statement of its own.

      Mathematics · no test exists

      Grounds that no rules can draw: equations and tiles

      In the book: some sets can be listed by rules while what they leave out can't be listed by any rules (section 7). The chapter gives no example.

      Now: there are concrete ones. Take equations built from whole numbers, such as x² + y² = z². If an equation has a whole-number solution, trying numbers one after another will find it, so the solvable equations can be listed. In 1970 Yuri Matiyasevich, finishing work by Martin Davis, Hilary Putnam and Julia Robinson, proved that no method lists the unsolvable ones. So there is no decision procedure for "does this equation have a solution?". Number theorists also use larger number systems, which add numbers such as √2 to the whole numbers. The same was known for only some of these until two teams proved it for every one (published 2025 and 2026). Escher's subject has its own case. Give a computer a set of square tiles with coloured edges and ask whether copies can cover an endless floor with touching edges matching. If they can't, a search finds a finite patch that is impossible to cover, so the sets that fail can be listed. The sets that succeed can't be: since the 1960s the question has been known to have no decision procedure.

      Why it matters: the "surprise" of section 7 is a fact about ordinary things, equations and tiles. With tiles the roles are swapped: failing to tile is the figure that a search can trace, and tiling is the ground.

      Mathematics · primes

      The test for primes became fast

      In the book: the primes are a figure too, so "is this number prime?" has a decision procedure. The one the P-system spells out checks every smaller number (section 9).

      Now: that check is hopeless for big numbers, and for a long time nobody had a test proved to be both always right and always fast. Three computer scientists in India, Manindra Agrawal, Neeraj Kayal and Nitin Saxena, found one (published 2004). Its work grows slowly: doubling the number of digits multiplies the work by at most a fixed amount. For numbers of special forms there are faster tests still. The largest prime known, found in October 2024, is 136,279,841 twos multiplied together, minus 1. It has 41,024,320 digits. A graphics chip, the kind of processor that also runs AI models, first reported it as "probably prime", which is not yet a proof. It was then confirmed by an exact test, run with several different programs on different machines.

      Why it matters: knowing that a ground is a figure is the start. How cheaply it can be drawn is a second question, and for the primes the answer turned out to be: far more cheaply than by the rules of section 9.
