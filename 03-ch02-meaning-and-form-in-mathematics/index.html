Dialogue
Two-Part Invention
1

What the Tortoise said to Achilles

The story. The race from the last dialogue is over. Achilles (the Greek hero, fastest runner alive) has caught the Tortoise and now sits comfortably on its shell. The Tortoise offers him a different kind of racecourse: one that most people think they can finish in two or three steps, but that really has no end.

The course is a tiny argument from the Greek geometer Euclid. It has two starting statements, called premises, and one statement that follows from them, the conclusion:

AThings that are equal to the same thing are equal to each other.
BThe two sides of this triangle are equal to the same thing.
ZSo the two sides of this triangle are equal to each other.

The Tortoise says: suppose I accept A and B, but I don't accept Z. Make me. Achilles replies that anyone who accepts A and B has to accept Z. Very well, says the Tortoise, that is a new statement. Write it in your notebook and call it C: "If A and B are true, Z must be true." Achilles writes it down. The Tortoise happily accepts C too, and still doesn't accept Z. What happens next is in section 2.

The form: a two-part invention

An invention is Bach's name for a short keyboard piece built from one small musical idea. In his fifteen two-part inventions (1723) there are just two voices, that is, two lines of melody played together, one by each hand. They take turns with the idea, each answering the other.

The dialogue has two voices too. The last one had three, but Zeno has left, and Achilles and the Tortoise pass one idea back and forth. Below is the opening of Bach's Invention No. 1: the upper voice plays a seven-note idea, and the lower voice answers with the same idea, lower down. Then they do it again, starting higher.

The hidden trickHofstadter didn't write this dialogue. It is "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles", published in 1895 by Lewis Carroll, the author of Alice in Wonderland, who was also a teacher of logic. The last page said Hofstadter borrowed his two characters from Carroll, and this is the piece he borrowed them from. Carroll ends it with two puns. The Tortoise asks Achilles to take the new name "Taught-Us", and Achilles asks the Tortoise to become "A Kill-Ease". Say them aloud: each is given the sound of the other's name.
2

The notebook that never fills up

Play Achilles. Each time the Tortoise accepts everything in the notebook and still refuses Z, tell him that he must accept Z now, and he will ask for that to be written down as well.

Premises the Tortoise accepts: 2Does he accept Z? Not yet

Every new line is true, and the Tortoise accepts every one. None of them helps. The line Achilles needs is always the next one. In Carroll's story the narrator leaves and comes back months later. Achilles is still writing, the Tortoise counts a thousand and one steps so far, and there are millions still to come.

It is Zeno's race again, moved from running to reasoning. On the last page Zeno's steps got shorter and shorter, so all of them fitted into 10 seconds. Here each premise is longer than the one before, so the notebook can never be finished. If every step of an argument first had to be written down and agreed to, no argument could ever reach its conclusion.

What went wrong?Carroll doesn't say. The answer most readers give since is this. An argument needs two different kinds of thing: statements you accept, and rules for getting from statements you accept to a new one. Logicians call such a rule a rule of inference. The Tortoise keeps turning the rule into one more statement. But a statement just sits on the page. Something still has to do the step, and that can't be written into the notebook, any more than a rulebook can make you obey it by adding the rule "obey this rulebook".

The formal systems of the last page are built to respect this difference. A formal system is a game played with rows of symbols. It has starting rows, called axioms, and it has rules for making new rows from old ones (each is called a rule of production). The rules are not rows. They are what you do to rows. A rule of production is a rule of inference with the meaning taken out.

The chapter now takes a new symbol game and asks where the meaning of its rows of symbols comes from. At its end it comes back to the Tortoise's challenge with a better question: can the steps of reasoning themselves be turned into the rules of such a game?

Chapter II
Meaning and Form in Mathematics
3

A new game: the pq-system

Words from the last page. A string is a row of symbols. A formal system is a game that makes strings: it gives you some strings to start with (axioms) and rules for making new strings from ones you have (each is a rule of production). Every string you can make this way is called a theorem of the system. The record of how you made it, one string per line, is its derivation.

Chapter II opens with a formal system even smaller than the MIU-system of the last page. Hofstadter calls it the pq-system. It has three symbols: the letter p, the letter q, and the hyphen -. For now they mean nothing. They are marks to be moved around.

The axioms
x p - q x -

Write some hyphens, then p, one hyphen, q, then the same hyphens as at the start plus one more.

In the pattern, x stands for any row of hyphens. Examples: -p-q-- and --p-q---
The one rule
x p y q z  →  x p y - q z -

If you have a theorem, you may add one hyphen just before the q and one hyphen at the very end.

Here x, y and z each stand for a row of hyphens. Example: from --p-q--- you get --p--q----

One thing is new. The MIU-system had a single axiom. Here there are infinitely many, one for every number of hyphens you start with. They can't be listed, so they are given by a pattern instead. A pattern for axioms like this is called an axiom schema.

Make some theorems. Each one you make is kept in the list on the right. Look for what they all have in common.

    Every theorem has three groups of hyphens: one before the p, one between p and q, and one after the q. The first two groups together have exactly as many hyphens as the third. In --p---q----- that is 2, 3 and 5.

    Why: every axiom has it (some hyphens and 1 on the left, the same number plus 1 on the right), and the rule adds one hyphen to the middle group and one to the last, so it never breaks it. And every string with this property can be made: start from the axiom with the right first group and apply the rule until the middle group is long enough.

    In short: a string is a theorem of the pq-system exactly when it has that shape and its first two hyphen groups add up to its third.

    4

    A test that always finishes

    The last page asked of the MIU-system: is there a decision procedure, a step-by-step test that is guaranteed to finish and say yes or no to "is this string a theorem?" There, the obvious test failed. Some rules made strings shorter, so a short string might be reached by a detour through long ones, and a search could never safely give up.

    The pq-system is easier, and the reason is worth more than the counting trick in section 3. Its only rule makes strings longer. So a theorem that isn't an axiom can only have come from a theorem two symbols shorter. That gives a test that works backwards:

    • 1Check the shape: hyphens, p, hyphens, q, hyphens. A string with the right shape is called well-formed. If the shape is wrong, the answer is no.
    • 2Is the string an axiom? Then yes.
    • 3Otherwise undo the rule: take one hyphen from the middle group and one from the end. Go back to step 2 with this shorter string. If there is nothing left to take, the answer is no.

    Each round the string gets shorter, so the test has to stop.

    or try:

      Hofstadter calls this working top-down: start from the string in question and take it apart. There is also a bottom-up way, which makes theorems instead of testing them. Picture a bucket. Throw in the shortest axiom. Then, round after round, apply the rule to everything in the bucket and throw in the next axiom. Every theorem lands in the bucket sooner or later. This works for any formal system; it is the row-by-row search of the last page. For most systems it is only a way of listing theorems. Here it is also a test, because nothing ever gets shorter: once everything new is longer than your string and your string hasn't appeared, it never will.

      5

      Where the meaning comes from

      Anyone who found the pattern in section 3 probably said it to themselves in words: "2 and 3 make 5". That is the step this chapter is about. Nothing in the rules mentions numbers or adding. The rules were fixed first, and they settled which strings are theorems. Only afterwards did we notice that the theorems look like sums.

      To say it carefully, Hofstadter uses two steps.

      • 1Match symbols with words. Read p as "plus", q as "equals", - as "one", -- as "two", and so on. Such a dictionary from symbols to words is called an interpretation. It turns --p---q----- into "2 plus 3 equals 5". Anyone can invent an interpretation. So far it is only a way of reading aloud.
      • 2Check that theorems match truths. Under this dictionary, every theorem reads as a true sum, and every well-formed string that is not a theorem reads as a false one. The strings the rules can make line up exactly with the true statements about adding two numbers.

      Two things whose parts line up like this, so that what happens in one is mirrored in the other, are called isomorphic, and the match between them is an isomorphism. The first page used a map and its city as the example. Here one side is the theorems of the pq-system and the other is a small piece of reality: the adding of whole numbers. Hofstadter's claim is that this match is what meaning is. The symbol p deserves to be read as "plus" because, when you read it that way, what the system does agrees with what adding does. It is step 2 that makes an interpretation meaningful. An interpretation that fails step 2 is meaningless, however sensible its words sound.

      Try four interpretations on the same seven strings. The "Theorem?" column comes from the rules alone and never changes. Watch whether "True?" agrees with it.

      The horse-and-apple reading is Hofstadter's own example of a meaningless interpretation: the dictionary is perfectly definite, but nothing lines up with anything. The "times" reading (added here, not from the book) is the more instructive failure. It produces proper arithmetic statements, and now and then a theorem even comes out true. But theorems and truths don't go together, so p does not mean "times".

      Hofstadter compares finding a meaningful interpretation to deciphering a lost ancient script. Nobody hands you the dictionary. You guess, test the guess against the texts, and you know you have it when everything suddenly makes sense. That is what happened in section 3.

      Form first, meaning secondThis is the "meaning and form" of the chapter's title. Which strings are theorems depends only on their form: their shape, and the rules. Meaning arrives later, when someone notices that the forms keep step with something in the world. Later chapters lean on this idea heavily, first to explain how a statement about numbers can also say something about itself, and then to ask whether meaning in a brain arises the same way.
      6

      Meaning that only watches

      In everyday language, meaning is active. Once we know what words mean, we use the meaning to make new true sentences. Knowing what "plus" and "equals" mean, anyone will agree to "2 plus 2 plus 2 plus 2 equals 8" at once.

      So it is tempting to say that this string must be a theorem of the pq-system:

      --p--p--p--q--------

      It isn't. It has three p's, so it is not even well-formed, and neither the axioms nor the rule can ever make it. The sentence it "says" is true, but that has no power over the system. In a formal system the meaning is passive: the rules alone decide what is a theorem, and the meaning only watches. We may read a theorem as a sum. We may not add a theorem because the sum is right. That would be changing the system, by adding a new rule or a new axiom.

      A second meaning

      Is "plus" and "equals" at least the meaning of the pq-system? No. Hofstadter gives another interpretation that passes the same test: read p as "equals" and q as "taken from".

      Every theorem is true under both readings, and under both, the well-formed strings that are not theorems come out false. Both interpretations are meaningful, and neither is the real one. A string of symbols doesn't carry one meaning inside it. It has as many meanings as there are parts of the world it lines up with.

      This will matter. The centre of the book is a statement about numbers which, read through a second, cleverly built interpretation, also says something about itself.

      7

      Do numbers match the world?

      The pq-system mirrors one small piece of reality. Hofstadter now asks how far that can go. Could all of reality be a formal system? In a very loose sense, maybe: think of the particles of the universe as the symbols, the laws of physics as the rules, and the state of everything at the beginning of time as the single axiom. But that is far too big to study, so he narrows the question to mathematics, where the mirroring is easiest to examine.

      Start with a fact everyone trusts: 12 × 12 = 144. Hardly anyone has checked it by laying out 12 rows of 12 things and counting them. Now take 987,654,321 × 123,456,789. Certainly nobody has ever counted that rectangle. A person with pencil and paper still gets the answer, 121,932,631,112,635,269, by shuffling digits according to rules learned at school, and we believe it completely.

      That is trust in a formal system. Long multiplication is symbol-pushing, and we trust it because its rules were built to mirror a few plain facts about counting that nobody doubts. For example: count a pile of things in any order, and you get the same number.

      The same 15 dots, counted as 3 rows of 5 or as 5 columns of 3. That is why 3 × 5 and 5 × 3 must agree, without counting. (An illustration for this page, not a figure from the book.)

      Hofstadter points out that such facts are so basic that they can't be proved from anything simpler. And they are not quite facts about the world. Put one raindrop on a windowpane next to another, and they run together: 1 + 1 = 1. Two clouds merge into one. Arithmetic is not about raindrops. It is about ideal numbers: things that stay separate, never merge and never split while being counted. Where the world behaves like that (coins, steps, votes) arithmetic fits it exactly. Where it doesn't, arithmetic is not wrong; we have picked the wrong piece of reality to line it up with.

      So "does this formal system mirror reality?" is never a yes-or-no matter about the symbols alone. It is the question from section 5 again: is there a part of reality that its theorems line up with?

      8

      One short proof, endless cases

      The basic facts of counting are simple. What is built on them is not. The chapter ends with a famous example, and it comes from Euclid, the source of the Tortoise's little argument. Around 300 BC he showed that the primes never run out. (A prime is a whole number bigger than 1 that only 1 and itself divide exactly: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11…) Nobody could check this by looking, because there are infinitely many numbers to look at. Here is the argument in the form Hofstadter gives it.

      • 1Pick any whole number. Call it N.
      • 2Multiply together all the numbers from 1 up to N. The result is written N! and called "N factorial". For N = 4 it is 1 × 2 × 3 × 4 = 24.
      • 3Add 1. For N = 4 that makes 25.
      • 4Divide this new number by any number from 2 up to N. There is always 1 left over, because N! itself is an exact multiple of each of them. (25 divided by 2, 3 or 4 leaves 1 each time.)
      • 5Every whole number bigger than 1 is either a prime or can be divided exactly by some prime. So N! + 1 has a prime that divides it, and by step 4 that prime is not among 2, 3, … N. It must be bigger than N. (For 25 it is 5.)
      • 6So whatever N is, there is a prime bigger than N. There is no biggest prime.

      Run the same argument for different N. Notice that N! + 1 is not always a prime itself (try 4 and 5). The argument never says it is. It only says that some prime bigger than N divides it.


      The slider stops at 18, and trying eighteen cases proves nothing about the nineteenth. The proof is the six lines above. Its power is in the words "any" and "whatever": the steps never use which number N is, so they hold for every N at once. A finite argument has settled infinitely many cases. Hofstadter calls this getting around infinity.

      Back to the TortoiseEach of the six steps feels forced. Once you grant the lines before, you can't refuse the next. That feeling is exactly what the Tortoise refused to have. The chapter closes by asking whether the steps of such reasoning can be written down as the rules of a formal system, so that a proof becomes a derivation and checking it becomes symbol-pushing that needs no feeling of being forced. Achilles tried to do this by adding premises, and it never ended. A formal system does it by building the steps in as rules. Whether that can capture all of reasoning about numbers is the question the next chapters work towards.
      9

      Recap: the dialogue and chapter in one breath

      Rules are something you do, not one more thing you say. And symbols pushed around by rules come to mean something when the strings the rules produce line up with truths about some part of the world.

      Dialogue

      Two voices, one endless notebook

      Carroll's Tortoise accepts every premise and asks for each step to be written down as another. A rule of reasoning has to be used, not just stated.

      Form

      The pq-system

      Infinitely many axioms from one pattern, and one rule that only lengthens. Because nothing shrinks, a test for theorems always finishes.

      Meaning

      An isomorphism, found afterwards

      Read p as plus and q as equals, and theorems match true sums. The meaning is passive, it isn't the only one, and it holds for ideal numbers, not raindrops.
