Introduction: A Musico-Logical Offering

GEB

A mathematician, an artist and a composer found the same strange pattern. A system goes up, level by level, and then it is at its start point again. Hofstadter gives this pattern the name Strange Loop. This chapter shows the Strange Loop. It also tells you that a mind can be a Strange Loop.

1

A king plays a tune, and Bach accepts the challenge

Bach In May 1747, Johann Sebastian Bach goes to the court of Frederick the Great of Prussia, in Potsdam. At that time, Bach is old and famous. Frederick plays the flute. He also collects new pianos (fortepianos).

The King plays a theme (the main melody of a piece of music) for Bach. The theme is long, and it has an unusual shape. The King tells Bach to make a fugue from the theme immediately.

What is a fugue? Music can have two or more melodies at the same time. Each separate melody line has the name voice, also when an instrument plays it. In a fugue, the voices start one after the other. Each voice starts with the same theme. Then the voices move around each other, and together they make one harmony. A "three-voice fugue" has three melody lines at the same time.

Bach plays a three-voice fugue immediately. Then the King asks for a six-voice fugue. Because this is very difficult without preparation, Bach does not do it. He goes back to Leipzig. Two months later, he sends the King a collection of pieces that all use the King's theme. The name of this collection is The Musical Offering.

IntuitionA six-voice fugue is like six conversations at the same time. Each conversation has its own meaning. Together, the six conversations make one harmony.

Bach liked games with words. At the start of the collection, he wrote a Latin sentence. The first letters of its words make a word. Click the sentence to see the word:

Regis Iussu Cantio Et Reliqua Canonica Arte Resoluta
RICERCAR

The sentence means: "At the King's command, the song and the remainder resolved with canonic art." (Section 2 tells you about canons.) Ricercar ("to seek") is an old name for a fugue. Thus, the sentence gives the name of the thing that it is. This is self-reference: a thing that talks about itself, or points back to itself. Self-reference is the main subject of all of the book.

2

Canons: one melody, copied and changed

The Musical Offering has many canons. A canon is the simplest musical "copy machine". One voice plays a theme. Then a second voice plays the same theme, a short time later. A round, for example "Frère Jacques", is a song of this type. The copies fit together, although they overlap.

Bach made the copy more complex. The copy can be different from the original. But a strict rule must connect the copy to the original:

Delay
start at a later time
Transpose
move higher or lower in pitch
Inversion
turn it upside down: each "up" becomes "down"
Retrograde
play it from the end to the start (a "crab canon")
Augmentation
make it slower: each note is two times longer
IntuitionA canon is a theme plus a rule. When you know the rule, the first voice contains the second voice. This is the start of the main subject of Hofstadter: one thing can contain the shape of a different thing. Hofstadter uses the word isomorphism for this. In an isomorphism, the parts of two things and their relations agree one-to-one. Thus, one thing can represent the other, like a map and the city on the map. The second voice of a canon is an isomorphic copy of the first voice.

3

The Endlessly Rising Canon: the first Strange Loop

One canon in the Offering has a special property. It goes higher and higher, but at the end, it is at its start point again. To understand this, you must know one music idea: key change. Key change uses two basic ideas: note names and keys.

Music basics: notes and keysDo you not know music notation? Open this panel for a short introduction with sound. If you know music notation, go to the next part.Click to close.
ANotes have letter names

The seven notes of do-re-mi (do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, si) also have letter names: C D E F G A B. C is do, D is re, E is mi, and the pattern continues. On a piano, these notes are the white keys.

The black keys are the notes between the white keys. Each black key has the name of a white key next to it. Sharp (♯) means "one piano key higher". Flat (♭) means "one piano key lower". Thus, the black key between C and D has two names: C♯ or D♭.

With the white keys and the black keys, there are 12 different notes. After B, the pattern starts again with a higher C. The distance from a note to the same note higher (for example, do at the top of do-re-mi) is an octave. A step of one piano key (white or black) is a semitone. A step of two piano keys is a whole tone.

Click a piano key to hear it. Play the white keys from left to right. You hear do-re-mi-fa-sol-la-si-do.

BA key is a home note plus a family of 7 notes

A key is the setting of a piece of music. A key has two parts:

  • a home note: the music goes back to this note frequently, and the music sounds complete on it;
  • a family of 7 notes (from the 12 notes). The music uses these notes most. When you play the family in sequence, it is a scale.

Do-re-mi-fa-sol-la-si-do from C uses only white keys: this is C major. Its home note is C, and its family sounds bright and happy. C minor has the same home note, C. But it replaces three notes (E, A and B) with the black keys immediately below them (E♭, A♭, B♭). This small change gives a darker, more serious sound. Bach's canon starts in C minor.

Summary: a key is a home note on which the music sounds complete, plus a family of 7 of the 12 notes. The music uses these 7 notes most. "C minor" means that the home note is C (do), with a minor family that sounds darker.

1Key change: move all of the tune

You can start a tune on a different home note, with the same pattern of steps. It is the same tune, but higher or lower, like a song with a higher voice. A move of the music to a new home note is a key change, or modulation.

Play the C minor tune. Then play the same tune from D (two piano keys higher). This puts the tune in D minor. On the keyboard, all of the family of notes moves two keys to the right.

2The trick of Bach: up through the keys

Now look at the canon of Bach, the Canon per Tonos ("canon through the keys"). Each time that the music plays, the key changes. It goes up a whole tone (two piano keys): C minor → D minor → E minor → F♯ minor → A♭ minor → B♭ minor. After six steps, the key is C minor again. The music goes up all the time, but it is at home again, one octave higher. Bach also wrote a note to the King: he hopes that the glory of the King goes up like the music.

Real music must stop at some point, because the notes become too high to hear. Shepard tones prevent this problem. Each note sounds in many octaves at the same time, and the highest and lowest octaves are quieter. Thus, your ear cannot tell which octave it hears. With Shepard tones, the loop can go up forever, but it never goes to a new place.

This is why the canon of Bach goes up one step at a time. Each step keeps 5 of the 7 notes, thus the change is very small to the ear. But after six small steps, the music goes through keys that have almost no notes in common, and then back home again.

DefinitionA hierarchy is a system with levels, one above the other, like the floors of a building or the ranks in an army. A Strange Loop occurs when you move up (or down) through the levels of a hierarchy. You do not expect it, but then you are at your start point again. In some hierarchies, the levels are not in a clear stack, and they loop into each other. Such a hierarchy is a Tangled Hierarchy.

Escher: Strange Loops that you can see

Escher The Dutch artist M. C. Escher made many pictures of Strange Loops:

  • Waterfall (1961): the water flows down a channel, and then it falls from the top of its own waterfall.
  • Ascending and Descending (1960): monks walk around a stairway on a roof. They always go up (or always go down), forever. This picture is the visual equivalent of the Endlessly Rising Canon.
  • Drawing Hands (1948): two hands, and each hand draws the other hand. Which hand is the "real" artist? Neither level is at the top.
  • Metamorphosis (1939–40, 1967–68): patterns that slowly change into other things. At the end, they change back into the first pattern.

All these pictures use the same trick: each small step looks correct. But when you follow all of the path, it is impossible. The canon is the same: each key change sounds correct, but all the changes together make a loop.

Intuition"Up" and "down" are different levels. "The artist" and "the drawing" are also different levels. In a Strange Loop, these levels tangle: the thing at the bottom supports the thing at the top.

Gödel: a Strange Loop in mathematics

Gödel The most important Strange Loop in the book is from Kurt Gödel. In 1931, he found a Strange Loop in mathematics. His idea starts from a very old puzzle. But first, you must know some words.

Words for this section.
  • A proof is a sequence of steps from initial assumptions to a conclusion. Each step obeys strict rules.
  • A formal system is a fixed list of initial statements (axioms), plus mechanical rules that make new statements (theorems). It is like a board game: the axioms are the start position, and the rules are the permitted moves.
  • A statement is provable if permitted moves can make it.
  • A system is consistent if it never proves a contradiction (a statement and its opposite).
  • A system is complete if it can prove each true statement in its language.

Step 1: The Liar (Epimenides paradox)

A paradox is a statement or an argument in which each step looks correct, but the result is a contradiction. Epimenides, an old poet from Crete, said: "All Cretans are liars." In its simplest form, the puzzle is this sentence:

"This sentence is false."

Step 2: The version of Gödel

Gödel replaced "false" with "unprovable". Then the paradox becomes a theorem.

"This statement has no proof in this system."

Step 3: How can mathematics talk about itself?

Step 2 has a problem. Mathematical formulas talk about numbers ("2 + 2 = 4", "7 is prime"). They cannot say "this statement", because statements are not numbers. Gödel solved this problem with a secret code, Gödel numbering: each formula becomes a number. Then a statement about numbers can also be a statement about formulas, and also about itself. The code has three small steps.

a) A very small mathematical language. Gödel used a basic language for number theory (the mathematics of the whole numbers 0, 1, 2, 3, …). It has the number 0, and a symbol S that means "the next number after". All other numbers use S symbols:

0 = 0S0 = 1SS0 = 2SSS0 = 3…

It also has the usual symbols +, × and =. Thus, the formula S0+S0=SS0 means "1 + 1 = 2".

b) Give each symbol a code number, as in a cipher with A = 1 and B = 2:

0 → 1S → 2= → 3+ → 4× → 5

Now each formula is a list of codes. For example, 0=0 becomes 1, 3, 1.

c) Put the list into one number. Gödel used the primes (whole numbers that you can divide only by 1 and by themselves: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, …). The 1st code becomes the power of 2, the 2nd code becomes the power of 3, and the 3rd code becomes the power of 5. The pattern continues for all the codes. (A power is a repeated multiplication: 33, "3 to the power 3", is 3 × 3 × 3 = 27.) Then multiply all the results together:

0=0  →  codes 1, 3, 1  →  21 × 33 × 51 = 2 × 27 × 5 = 270

Thus, the Gödel number of the formula 0=0 is 270. The code also works in the opposite direction. You can divide each whole number into primes in only one way (270 = 2 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 5). Thus, from 270 you can find the powers 1, 3, 1, and then the formula 0=0 again. No two formulas have the same number.

Step 4: Back to Step 2: make "I have no proof" from numbers

Step 2 needs a sentence that says "this statement has no proof". With Gödel numbering, Gödel can make this sentence. He uses three steps.

1. "Has a proof" becomes a question about numbers. A proof is a list of formulas, thus it also has a Gödel number. To check a proof, you check that each line obeys the rules. When all the parts are numbers, this check is usual arithmetic: you decode numbers and compare them. Thus, "the formula with Gödel number n has a proof" is also a formula of number theory (a very long formula), like "n is even".

2. Thus, number theory can say "the formula with number n has no proof", for each number n.

3. Make n the number of the formula itself. This is the clever part. A formula cannot simply contain its own number. If you write the number into the formula, the formula changes, and then its number changes. It is like a photo of a page, printed on that same page. The solution of Gödel is to describe the number, not to write it: "take this template, put its own number into it, and the result has no proof".

The same trick works in English:

"is unprovable when written after its own quotation" is unprovable when written after its own quotation.

Gödel did the same thing with numbers. The result is a formula of number theory, with the name G. When you decode G, it says "G has no proof". This is the sentence from Step 2, but now it is real mathematics. Thus, the reasoning of Step 2 applies to it.

If the system can prove G, it proves a false statement. Thus, a trustworthy system cannot prove G. Because G has no proof, its statement is true. G is true, but it has no proof.

The result: Gödel's Incompleteness Theorem(In usual mathematics, a theorem is a result with a proof.) Each consistent formal system that can do arithmetic contains statements that are true, but that the system cannot prove. Gödel made a statement with two levels of meaning. On one level, it is about numbers. On the other level, it says "I am not provable", and this is true. Mathematics can never fully contain itself.
6

How mathematics went into this trap

The result of Gödel was the end of a goal that was 2,000 years old. The goal was fully rigorous mathematics: each fact has a step-by-step proof from a few clear initial rules, with no gaps and no contradictions. Hofstadter tells this history briefly. Each step had the purpose to make mathematics safer, but each step caused a new problem.

  1. ~300 BC

    Euclid: mathematics from a few axioms

    Euclid made all of geometry from a small number of axioms, one step at a time. This became the model for all mathematics.

  2. 1800s

    Other geometries: axioms are choices

    Mathematicians changed one axiom of Euclid, the parallel postulate. This axiom says: through a point next to a line, there is only one line that never meets that line. The result was new geometries that also work correctly. Thus, axioms are not obvious facts. They are choices. Question: if axioms are only choices, how do we know that they never cause a contradiction?

  3. 1870s–90s

    Cantor: sets are the base of all mathematics

    A set is a collection of things: for example, the set of all cats, or the set of even numbers. The set theory of Georg Cantor became the base of all mathematics, also of infinity. But a set can contain other sets. Question: can a set contain itself?

  4. 1901

    Russell's paradox: self-reference breaks sets

    Some sets contain themselves. The set of "all things that are not cats" is not a cat, thus it is one of its own members. Most sets do not contain themselves: the set of cats is not a cat. Bertrand Russell asked about the set of all sets that do not contain themselves. Does it contain itself?

    If yes, it does not obey its own rule, thus the answer is no. If no, it obeys the rule, thus the answer is yes. This is a contradiction at the base of mathematics. Here is the same trap in a usual example:

    A barber in a village shaves exactly the men who do not shave themselves. Does the barber shave himself?

  5. 1910–13

    Principia Mathematica: stop the loop

    Russell and Alfred North Whitehead made mathematics again, with a strict ladder of levels. Usual objects are on the bottom rung. Sets of objects are on the next rung, and sets of sets are above them. A set can contain only things from lower rungs. Thus, nothing can contain itself or talk about itself, and you cannot even write Russell's paradox. Question: is this system safe and complete?

  6. 1920s

    Hilbert: prove that it is perfect

    David Hilbert asked mathematicians to prove two properties of such a system. It is consistent (it never proves a contradiction). It is complete (it can prove each true statement in its language).

  7. 1931

    Gödel: the loop comes back

    Gödel showed that the two hopes fail. With Gödel numbering (section 5), usual arithmetic can talk about itself. Thus, self-reference comes back, and it obeys all the rules of the ladder. Each consistent system that can do arithmetic has true statements that it cannot prove (it is incomplete). It also cannot prove that it is consistent. Nobody can stop the loop, because it is a part of the numbers.

  8. 1936

    Church and Turing: the limits of machines

    Alonzo Church and Alan Turing found the same type of limit for each step-by-step mechanical procedure. A computer program is such a procedure. No program can answer some yes-or-no questions. Thus, machines become a part of the story.

7

Can a machine that obeys rules have a mind?

Now, the real question of the book. In the 1800s, Charles Babbage designed mechanical computers. Ada Lovelace saw that these machines can operate on all types of symbols, not only on numbers. But she said that a machine can do only the things that you tell it to do. One hundred years later, Alan Turing asked directly: can machines think?

This is the puzzle. A computer always obeys its rules. It is rigid and literal, and it never becomes bored. Minds look like the opposite. Intelligence must be able to do these things:

  • change its response to new situations
  • use accidents that give good results
  • understand messages that are not clear, or that contradict themselves
  • find the most important thing
  • see what is the same in things that are different, and what is different in things that are the same
  • make new ideas

How can rigid rules make this behavior? The answer of Hofstadter is levels. In your brain, each neuron (brain cell) is a simple cell that obeys chemistry: it fires, or it does not fire. No single neuron understands anything. But billions of neurons together make thoughts, jokes and ideas. Rigid rules are at the low level, and flexible meaning is at the high level.

Sometimes the top level loops back down. Your thoughts can be about your own thoughts, and the idea of "me" can change what your neurons do next. This is a Strange Loop. The theory of Hofstadter is that this loop is an "I".

This is why Gödel is in the book. His sentence G has the same shape. At the bottom, there are symbols with no meaning, and rules move them. At the top, there is a meaning ("I have no proof") that points back at the sentence itself. Gödel showed that such a loop can grow from usual arithmetic. Hofstadter's theory is that the same thing occurs in a brain.

Read from the bottom to the top. Parts that obey rules make patterns. The patterns have meaning. Then the meaning describes the system itself. The gold arrow is the loop.

The structure of the book

GEB has Chapters (explanations) and Dialogues (stories with humor), one after the other. The characters in the Dialogues are Achilles and the Tortoise. They come from Zeno's paradoxes (old Greek puzzles: one puzzle "proves" that fast Achilles can never catch a tortoise that starts in front of him). They also come from a short text by Lewis Carroll, What the Tortoise Said to Achilles. Later, a Crab, a Genie and other characters also join them.

Many Dialogues have the shape of a piece by Bach. Some Dialogues are canons or fugues in words: the characters repeat, invert or reverse the lines of the other characters. Each Dialogue shows an idea as a story. Then the next Chapter explains the idea.

Advice for the readerDo not worry if a Dialogue does not make sense at first. Usually, it is a puzzle box, and the next Chapter gives you the key. The structure of the book is also an example of its subject: the form is a mirror of the content.

Summary of the chapter

Bach, Escher and Gödel each made a system that goes up through levels and loops back to itself. The theory of Hofstadter is that the same Strange Loop lets rules with no meaning make a mind.

Bach

Music

Canons copy a theme with a rule. The Endlessly Rising Canon goes up forever, but it goes back home.

Escher

Pictures

Waterfalls, stairways and hands that loop. Each step is correct, but the full picture is impossible.

Gödel

Logic

Code statements as numbers. Then mathematics can talk about itself: "I am unprovable." Thus, each system has truths that it cannot prove.

10

Modern view: what we know now

As of November 2026

GEB was published in 1979. After 1979, machines that learn from data let researchers test some of the large "what if" questions of this chapter. This section tells you the current status of the ideas of the chapter.

AI

Machines that obey rules now do much of the list of Hofstadter

In the book: how can rigid rules make flexible intelligence? Intelligence must understand unclear messages, find what is important, see what is the same, and make new ideas.

Now: Large language models (the AI systems in chatbots such as ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude) do many of these things well. Inside, they are only arithmetic, repeated billions of times: rigid rules at the bottom, and flexible behavior at the top. In 2025, two of these models received gold-medal results at the International Mathematical Olympiad, the most difficult mathematics contest for school students. They wrote their proofs in usual language. Hofstadter was skeptical of such systems for a long time, but in 2023 he said that they changed his opinions. He said that they support the idea that human creativity comes from "the brain's hardware".

Why it is important: the main theory of the book is that meaning can grow from rules with no meaning. Now there are examples that work, and researchers can study them. People still do not agree about one question: do these systems understand?

AI · interpretability

Levels that you can see in a machine

In the book: meaning is in patterns at a high level. You cannot find a thought in one single low-level part.

Now: a new field, mechanistic interpretability, examines the inside of AI models to find how they work. It is like neuroscience for machines. MIT Technology Review put it in its list of ten breakthrough technologies of 2026. One of its important results agrees closely with the book. A single artificial neuron usually has many unrelated meanings. For example, one neuron in an image-recognition model responds to the faces of cats and also to the fronts of cars.

Real concepts are patterns across many neurons. These patterns have the name features. Now researchers can follow chains of features while a model operates. For example, a model must find the capital of the state that contains Dallas. Inside, it first activates "Texas" and then "Austin". When it writes two lines that rhyme, it selects the last word before it writes the line.

Why it is important: the levels of Hofstadter are not only a metaphor now. In AI models, we can see meaning that occurs above the level of the parts. But the current tools show only a part of what occurs.

AI · self-reference

Can a loop examine itself?

In the book: a self (an "I") can be a Strange Loop: a system with high-level patterns that represent the system itself.

Now: in October 2025, researchers at Anthropic did a test. The question was: can a language model find changes in its own internal state? The researchers put a concept directly into the internal activity of the model, for example the pattern for "ALL CAPS" text. Then they asked the model if it found something unusual. Sometimes, the most capable models found it and named the concept (here: a word like "loud" or "shout"). They did this before the concept was in any text that they wrote.

This worked only approximately 20% of the time. The researchers say clearly that this result tells us nothing about consciousness in the model.

Why it is important: can machines make loops that model themselves? Researchers can now test this question in a laboratory. Before, people could only argue about it.

Mathematics

The goal of Hilbert is half complete, and the result of Gödel is still correct

In the book: Hilbert hoped to make mathematics fully mechanical and complete. Gödel (and later Turing) showed that no fixed system can contain every truth.

Now: proof assistants such as Lean use a machine to check each step of a proof. This is the idea of Hilbert (a fully checkable proof), in practice. AI systems now work with proof assistants. In October 2026, the journal Science reported results from AlphaProof Nexus by Google DeepMind. It made machine-checked solutions to 9 problems from the mathematician Paul Erdős that nobody solved before, and to 44 open conjectures about number sequences. Mathematicians still argue about claims for much larger problems, for example the 2026 result from OpenAI on the Navier–Stokes equations of fluid flow.

Why it is important: the theorem of Gödel is still correct, because these AI provers also work in formal systems. Thus, there will always be true statements that they cannot prove. The theorem does not prevent mathematics by machines. It says that no single fixed system (human or machine) can find all of mathematics. Some people say that the theorem of Gödel proves that minds cannot be machines. Most logicians, and also Hofstadter, do not agree with this argument.
