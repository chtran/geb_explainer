Gödel, Escher, Bach · Explainer 05

Contracrostipunctus + Consistency, Completeness, and Geometry

GEB

A Tortoise keeps bringing his friend a record that shakes the friend's record player to pieces, however good the player. Hofstadter uses the story as a picture of Gödel's theorem, which says that no fixed set of rules that never contradicts itself, and that is strong enough to handle addition and multiplication, can produce every true statement about numbers. This page covers the dialogue Contracrostipunctus and Chapter IV, which asks what it means for a system of rules to be consistent and complete, and finds the answer in a 2,000-year argument about parallel lines.

1

The Tortoise, the Crab, and a goblet with four letters on it

The story. Achilles visits the Tortoise, who tells him about a long contest with his friend the Crab. The Crab had bought a record player that the salesman called Perfect: it could reproduce any sound at all. The Tortoise came to visit with a record titled "I Cannot Be Played on Record Player 1". A few notes in, the player began to shake, and then it fell to pieces. The Tortoise had studied how the player was built and had put on the record exactly the sounds that would make it rattle itself apart.

The Crab bought a better player. The Tortoise came back with "I Cannot Be Played on Record Player 2". And so on. In the end the Crab orders a player that looks at each record first and rebuilds itself to suit it. Does that one win? The Tortoise won't say. He only asks whether Achilles knows Gödel's theorem.

Then the roles turn around. As Achilles gets up to leave, he gives the Tortoise a present: a goblet, said to be the last piece blown by J. S. Bach himself, who made glass as a hobby, with the letters B, A, C, H cut into it. The Tortoise admits that he has a search of his own, for a Perfect goblet. He notices that the four letters are also the names of four notes (musicians name notes by letters), picks up his violin and plays them. On the last note the goblet shatters, and the dialogue stops dead. The goblet has met the Crab's fate: it was broken by a tune made for it, the one etched into its own glass. This time the Tortoise is the one left holding the pieces.

The form: Bach's last fugue

A fugue is a piece where several voices (separate melody lines) enter one after another with the same tune and weave together. The craft of fitting melodies against each other is called counterpoint. In his last years Bach wrote The Art of Fugue, a set of fugues and canons all built on one tune, and gave each fugue the title Contrapunctus, Latin for counterpoint.

The last one is unfinished. Near the point where the manuscript breaks off, Bach brings in a new tune made of his own name. Musicians name notes by letters. In the German way of naming them, which has an H where English has a B, four notes that sit side by side on the piano are called B, A, C and H. So Bach's name is a four-note tune:

In the dialogue the Tortoise says this tune is the same played upside down (every step up becomes a step down) and backwards (last note first). Is he right? Press the second button.

The hidden trickThe title joins Contrapunctus to acrostic, a text whose first letters spell a message. Take the first letter of each speech in the dialogue, in order, and they spell a sentence. The dialogue drops a hint: Achilles remarks, in passing, that Lewis Carroll liked to hide words in the first letters of his poems. The sentence says what it hides. To find it, read the first letters of its first six words from right to left.
Hofstadter's Contracrostipunctus Acrostically Backwards Spells 'J. S. Bach'

Those letters are H, C, A, B, S, J. Backwards, that is J, S, B, A, C, H.

So the dialogue signs itself the way Bach signed his fugue: with a name hidden in the material. The story's three objects are all undone by something that points back at themselves: the record names its player, the goblet carries its own tune, and the fugue, in the manuscript we have, breaks off just after its composer writes his name into it. Pointing back at oneself is called self-reference, and the whole book is built on it. And the dialogue ends as the fugue does, by breaking off in the middle. Just before, the Tortoise had described how Bach's last fugue stops without warning, followed by dead silence. The dialogue's closing stage direction uses the same words.

2

The record-player game

Play the Tortoise's game. A high-fidelity player (one that reproduces sound faithfully) plays ordinary records perfectly. A cheap low-fidelity player can only manage some of the notes. Try each record on each player.

Player
Record

The tunes, and each player's "weak note", are made up for this demo. The dialogue doesn't give that detail.

Play on until every row of the table says "No". The rows get there for two different reasons. The cheap player is safe from the Tortoise, but only because it can't make the sounds that would hurt it, and it can't make many other sounds either. Each high-fidelity player is good enough to start making the sounds that destroy it, so there is one sound it never finishes reproducing: the one on its own record. And if you buy Record Player 2 and give it the record that broke Record Player 1, it plays without trouble. No record is unplayable in general. Each one is aimed at a single machine.

The Tortoise's moral is that the fault isn't in any one player. It is in the hope for a Perfect one. Mathematicians once had the same hope: the Introduction told how, in the early 1900s, they set out to build one perfect system of rules for all of mathematics.

The idea the dialogue sets upGödel's theorem ended that hope: in any formal system (starting statements plus mechanical rules for making new ones) that is strong enough to do addition and multiplication of whole numbers, and that never contradicts itself, there is a true statement the rules can never produce. The Tortoise's game is a picture of that theorem, with record players standing for formal systems. The chapter now asks what the picture shows: what it means for a formal system to be consistent (roughly: it never contradicts itself), what it means for it to be complete (roughly: it can produce every true statement), and what it is about a strong one that keeps it from being Perfect.
Chapter IV
Consistency, Completeness, and Geometry
3

One object, two meanings

A record's grooves have an obvious meaning: music. That is the meaning the record was made for, and Hofstadter calls it the explicit meaning. The Tortoise's records carry a second one. The same grooves, played on one particular machine, are a set of instructions for shaking that machine apart. Nobody would see this by listening. It is an implicit meaning, and it only appears when you line the sounds up against the design of the player.

The book has a word for lining things up like this. An isomorphism is a match between two things whose parts correspond one-to-one, like a map and its city.

Chapter II used one to give meaning to a tiny formal system, the pq-system. Its strings are made of hyphens, one p and one q. Here is the whole system: a pattern for its axioms (the strings you are given for free) and one rule.

Axioms

Any string of the shape xp-qx-, where x stands for any number of hyphens (at least one), the same number both times. For example --p-q---.

Rule

From a theorem, make a new one by adding one hyphen just before the q and one at the very end. From --p-q--- you get --p--q----.

The strings the rule can reach from the axioms are the system's theorems. --p---q----- is one. The symbols mean nothing by themselves. But read p as "plus", q as "equals" and each group of hyphens as a number, and that theorem says "2 plus 3 equals 5". A way of reading symbols like this is called an interpretation. This one fits: the axioms say "x plus 1 equals x + 1" and the rule keeps both sides in step, so every theorem turns into a true addition.

The chapter's first point is that one object can have several interpretations at once, each through a different isomorphism. The dialogue itself is an example. On the surface it is a story. Its two halves match each other: record player and goblet, record and violin tune, Crab and Tortoise. Its first letters spell a message. And the whole thing lines up with Gödel's theorem. A string states something, as a record holds a sound. Playing a record through stands for the system producing that string as a theorem. One item comes from the Introduction: Gödel's string, a string of a system's own symbols that says, in effect, "I am not a theorem of this system".

The player falling to pieces has no exact partner, since a formal system doesn't break. And the story has no picture of a system that contradicts itself. That is the next topic.

Gödel's string has two meanings in the same way the record has. Read one way it is a plain statement about numbers. Read another way it talks about the system it is written in. Later chapters build it; this one prepares the ground by asking how symbols get meanings at all, and what can go wrong.

4

Break a system, then mend it without touching it

1A reading that fits

Take the pq-system from section 3, with p read as "plus" and q as "equals". Every theorem is a true addition. System and reading fit.

2Add an axiom, and the reading breaks

Now Hofstadter adds a second pattern of axioms: any string of the shape xp-qx, one hyphen shorter at the end. That makes --p-q-- a theorem. Read the old way it says "2 plus 1 equals 2".

The grid below shows the damage. Each cell is one string: the row gives the two groups before the q, the column gives the number of hyphens after it. Turn the extra axioms on, then try the other reading of q.

With the extra axioms and the reading "equals", some theorems come out false. (When a system produces a string as a theorem, mathematicians say it proves the statement the string stands for. So this system proves false things.) Its theorems even disagree with each other: "2 plus 1 equals 2" and "2 plus 1 equals 3" can't both hold. A system that does this, under a given reading, is called inconsistent.

3Change the reading, and the clash is gone

But look at what the other reading did. Nothing about the system changed: same axioms, same rule, same theorems. Read q as "is at least" (so --p-q-- says "2 plus 1 is at least 2") and every theorem is true again. The contradiction was never in the strings. It was between the strings and the words we had attached to them.

Consistency needs an interpretationA formal system by itself is just strings; strings can't contradict each other. A system together with an interpretation is consistent when every theorem comes out as a true statement, and inconsistent when at least one comes out false. Change the interpretation and the verdict can change. (The Introduction's short version, "never contradicts itself", is the most visible case: if two theorems contradict each other, one of them must be false.)

Notice also that swapping the meaning of q is something no rule of the system could do. You did it from outside, the way Chapter I's puzzle was settled by stepping outside the rules.

5

The same thing happened to geometry

Around 300 BC the Greek mathematician Euclid wrote the Elements, which derives all of geometry from a few starting statements. He called them postulates; they are axioms under an older name. There are five. In plain words:

  1. A straight line can be drawn between any two points.
  2. A straight line can be extended as far as you like.
  3. A circle can be drawn around any point, with any size.
  4. All right angles (square corners) are equal.
  5. Through a point beside a line, exactly one line can be drawn that never meets the first. (Two lines that never meet are called parallel, so this is the parallel postulate. The wording is a later, simpler one.)

The first four are short and feel obvious. The fifth is different. Euclid's own wording of it runs to several lines, and it makes a claim about what happens infinitely far away. Euclid himself put off using it for as long as he could. For two thousand years, mathematicians tried to show it wasn't needed as an axiom, by proving it from the other four.

In 1733 an Italian priest, Girolamo Saccheri, tried a new route. He assumed the fifth postulate was false and worked out what followed, expecting to hit a contradiction. That would prove the postulate had to be true. He derived one strange result after another. No contradiction came. Finally he reached a result so odd (two straight lines that draw closer and closer for ever without meeting) that he declared it against the very nature of a straight line, stopped, and published his book as a defence of Euclid.

He had found a new geometry and thrown it away. About a century later, in the 1820s and 1830s, János Bolyai in Hungary and Nikolai Lobachevsky in Russia each did the same work, independently, and drew the other conclusion: the strange results fit together and never clash, so they describe a geometry of their own. Geometries where the fifth postulate fails are called non-Euclidean.

What could such a geometry look like? Try three worlds. In each, L is a line and P is a point beside it. Turn a line through P and watch whether it meets L.

World

On flat paper the answer is Euclid's: one line misses. On the sphere, none does. On the saddle-shaped surface, a whole fan of lines misses. Both curved worlds read "line" as "straightest path". With that reading, the first four postulates hold on the saddle as well as on flat paper, so they don't decide between the two. The sphere is a harder case. It needs more words read in a new way, and that is the next section.

6

What does "line" mean?

You may have objected to the last demo: those curves on the sphere and the saddle aren't straight. That objection is exactly what stopped Saccheri. The word "line" came to him with a picture attached, and when his results stopped fitting the picture he trusted the picture.

But look at what Euclid's postulates actually pin down. They never say what a point or a line is. (Euclid did offer definitions, but they are too vague to settle anything. Only the postulates do any work.) They only say how points and lines behave toward each other: two points have a line through them, lines can be extended, and so on. "Point" and "line" are undefined terms. Like p and q, they are symbols, and anything that behaves as the postulates demand is entitled to the names. The pq-system carries the same warning: it is easy to read "plus" into p and then believe things about p that the rules never said.

On a sphere, the shortest path between two places runs along a great circle, a circle as big as the sphere allows, like the equator. Planes fly along them. So read "line" as "great circle". One problem remains, and the drawing shows it.

So the mending takes three re-readings. "Line" means great circle. "Point" means a pair of opposite spots. And "extended as far as you like" in the second postulate means "never comes to an end": a great circle goes round and never ends, though it isn't infinitely long. With those, two points fix one line again, and every two lines meet in exactly one point, so there are no parallels at all. It is the move from section 4. There, a new axiom clashed with the old reading of q, and a new reading removed the clash. Here, a new fifth postulate ("through a point beside a line, no line can be drawn that never meets the first") clashes with the old reading of "line", and a new reading removes it.

One more piece of the picture. Drop the fifth postulate and keep the other four, and you have a smaller geometry, called absolute geometry. Everything it proves holds on flat paper and on the saddle alike, since both obey those four as read. The sphere obeys them only after its three re-readings, so it is a rival geometry too, but not one built on this same core. Absolute geometry is the shared core. It can't settle the fifth postulate either way, so geometry forks: add the fifth and you get Euclid's geometry, add a rival and you get another.

What geometry learnedThere isn't one true geometry that mathematics can prove. There are several, each consistent, and the fifth postulate is a choice between them. Which one describes the space we live in is a question for measurement, not proof. It became a question for physics, and physics has since found that space is very slightly curved near heavy objects such as the Sun.
7

Kinds of consistency

1Agreeing with the world, or with each other

Section 4 called a system consistent when every theorem "comes out true". True of what? Hofstadter splits that definition in two.

External consistency: the theorems agree with the world. Internal consistency: the theorems agree with each other. The second is the one that matters in mathematics, and it has a neat form: a system (with its interpretation) is internally consistent if you can imagine a world in which all its theorems are true together. Non-Euclidean geometry clashed with everyone's picture of the real world, yet it was internally consistent, because there are worlds (a sphere, a saddle) where it holds.

Hofstadter's example has just three theorems: TbZ, ZbE and EbT. Let T, Z and E be the Tortoise, Zeno (an ancient Greek philosopher who turns up in the book's dialogues) and a third character, Egbert. Choose a reading for b.

Under the second reading, each theorem on its own could be true. Only the three together are impossible. Inconsistency can hide in the combination.

2A clash you can look at

Escher's Relativity is a picture of a clash that hides in the combination, like the three theorems above. In this 1953 lithograph (a print made from a drawing on stone), people climb staircases in one building, but "down" points in three different directions. Each staircase with its walker makes sense. The building itself could be built. A world in which all those people walk it at once could not exist: gravity would have to pull three ways. See the print at mcescher.com.

Hofstadter's point is about us, the viewers. We recognise a staircase instantly, and once we have, we can't un-see it. So when the whole picture clashes, we don't escape by deciding the staircases are something else. We keep the small, familiar readings and accept an impossible world. It is Saccheri's problem in a picture: a familiar meaning that won't let go, even when letting go would dissolve the clash.

3Degrees of consistency

"Imaginable" comes in degrees, so consistency does too. Hofstadter sorts imagined worlds by which rules they are allowed to break:

  • Logically consistent: no statement is both true and false. Almost any story passes.
  • Mathematically consistent: it also keeps the facts of mathematics. For example, a world where 2 plus 1 equals 2 fails here.
  • Physically consistent: it also keeps the laws of physics. For example, a story with a machine that runs for ever without fuel fails here.
  • Biologically consistent: it also keeps the facts of biology. For example, a story with a talking tortoise fails here.

From here on, "consistent" means internally consistent: some imaginable world makes all the theorems true together. For a system meant to describe whole numbers, the intended world is the ordinary numbers, and a theorem that is false of the ordinary numbers is the clearest sign of trouble, so section 4's test, "every theorem is true", is the one we keep using. Section 8 shows where the two can come apart.

The list raises a question. Logic must be the same in every world we can talk about sensibly. Geometry, as sections 5 and 6 showed, need not be. What about number theory: could there be worlds with different arithmetic? The answer needs one more idea, completeness.

8

Completeness, and the limit on strong systems

1The definition

Consistency, as section 4 defined it, asks: is every theorem true? Turn the question around and you get the other property. A system with an interpretation is complete when every true statement that can be written in the system's symbols is a theorem.

Hofstadter puts the pair like this. Consistency is the least you need before symbols can be said to mean anything: whatever the system produces is true. Completeness is the most you could hope for: whatever is true, the system produces.

The limit "that can be written in the system's symbols" matters. The original pq-system is complete, yet it knows nothing about multiplication or even "2 plus 3 plus 4". Its strings can only say that two numbers add up to a third, and it produces every true string of that kind. That is all completeness asks.

2A consistent system with a gap

Here is the grid again, now with both verdicts. The extra axioms stay on, and it starts with q read as "is at least". A dashed cell is a true statement that is not a theorem.

Reading q as "is at least" made the system consistent, but it also made lots of new statements true, such as "2 plus 2 is at least 1", and the system proves only a few of them. The dashed cells are truths the system can't reach. There are two ways to close such a gap:

  • Add rules, so the system proves more.
  • Tighten the interpretation, so fewer statements count as true. The third reading in the grid, "equals, or is 1 more than", does this, and the gap closes.
3Number theory can't close its gap

For this toy system, tightening is the sensible choice. For number theory it isn't on offer. Its symbols are meant to say plus, times and equals; read them as anything looser and you are no longer doing number theory. That leaves adding rules.

And here is the Tortoise again. Gödel showed that whenever a consistent system is strong enough to do addition and multiplication of whole numbers, there is a true statement it can't prove: its Gödel string, which says "I am not a theorem". The statement has to be true. If it were a theorem, the system would have proved something false. Gödel's method, as the Introduction sketched, was to code statements as numbers, so that a system about numbers can make statements about itself (self-reference again). The proof itself comes in later chapters.

Add that statement as a new axiom and you have a new, stronger system, which has a new unprovable truth of its own. Each new record player gets its own record. What about the Crab's last player, the one that rebuilds itself? The dialogue leaves that open, and so does this chapter. The book returns to it in Chapter XV.

4Why geometry is in a chapter about Gödel

Section 7 asked whether there could be worlds with different arithmetic. The chapter's answer is yes, in the way geometry allowed it. Number theory has a shared core, like absolute geometry. Gödel's theorem will show that the core leaves some statements undecided, as the four postulates leave the fifth. Add such a statement, or add its opposite, and you get rival number theories. The rivals agree on every ordinary sum: 2 plus 1 is 3 in all of them. They differ only on unusual statements like Gödel's.

How can the opposite of a true statement be added without a contradiction? By the move from sections 4 and 6. The rival system is internally consistent under a different reading of "number", as the sphere was under a different reading of "line". What such numbers are like is a puzzle the book takes up in Chapter XIV.

Back to the record playersA weak system is like the cheap player. It can be complete about the little it can say, as the pq-system is, but most truths about numbers can't even be written in it. A strong system is like the high-fidelity player. It can state every truth about numbers, and that same power lets it describe itself, which is what allows a statement to be aimed at it. Neither is a Perfect system for number theory, and no consistent system can be.
9

Recap: the dialogue and chapter in one breath

Symbols mean only what a matching with some world, real or imagined, lets them mean. Choose the matching and you decide whether a system is consistent, and whether it is complete. For a system strong enough to do addition and multiplication of whole numbers, and read as arithmetic, you can't have both.

Dialogue

A record for every player

Each good player is broken by a record aimed at it; the cheap one is safe only because it can do so little. The goblet replays the trick on the Tortoise, and the first letters hide a name, as Bach's last fugue does.

Formal systems

Consistent and complete

Consistent: every theorem is true under the interpretation. Complete: every truth the symbols can state is a theorem. Both depend on how the symbols are read.

Geometry

"Line" is an undefined term

Deny Euclid's fifth postulate and no contradiction follows, only new geometries. A clash between axioms and meanings can be cured by changing the meanings. The other four postulates can't settle the fifth, as the core of number theory can't settle Gödel's statement.

10

Modern view: what we know now

As of November 2026

The Tortoise's game is now played for real, against AI systems and against the programs that check mathematical proofs. And the question this chapter handed to physics, which geometry space has, is still being measured.

AI · security

The record-player game, played against AI

In the book: every good record player has a record made to break it, and each better player gets a new record of its own (section 2).

Now: AI systems meet such "records" all the time. In a study published in 2023, researchers trained a program (training means teaching by practice, not by written rules) whose only job was to beat KataGo, an AI that plays the board game Go far better than any human. It won more than 97% of its games, not by playing Go well but by tricking KataGo into serious blunders.

AI systems built on language show the same pattern. A jailbreak is a message written to make a large language model break its own safety rules. In a study presented in 2026, researchers from Anthropic, Google DeepMind, OpenAI, universities and other groups took 12 published defences against jailbreaks and against prompt injections (text hidden in a web page or email that hijacks an AI assistant), most of which had reported that almost no attacks got through, and built attacks aimed at each one. For most of the 12, more than 90% of those attacks succeeded.

A cousin of the Crab's last idea, a player that looks at each record first, is also in use: Anthropic puts a second AI in front of its chatbot to screen what goes in and out. The company says itself that no AI system on the market has perfectly robust defences.

Why it matters: the Tortoise's pattern holds in practice: defend against one record, and a new one is made for the new machine. But this is experience, not proof. Gödel proved that no consistent strong formal system can be Perfect; nobody has proved that a well-defended AI is impossible.

AI · hidden meaning

Lists of numbers that carry a second message, for one machine only

In the book: the same grooves are music to a listener and, to one particular player, instructions for shaking itself apart (section 3).

Now: a study published in the science journal Nature in April 2026 found a double meaning of this kind in AI training data (the text an AI learns from). The researchers gave a "teacher" language model a trait, such as a fondness for owls, and had it write nothing but lists of numbers. A second, "student" model was then trained on those lists. Afterwards the student picked the owl as its favourite animal more than 60% of the time, up from 12%. Nothing in the lists mentions owls. And the hidden message is aimed, like the Tortoise's records: it got through only when teacher and student were built on the same underlying model, as adjusted copies of one original (or on ones that behave alike). A student built on a different model read the same lists and showed no reliable effect.

Why it matters: an implicit meaning doesn't sit in the symbols alone. It appears when one particular machine reads them. That was Hofstadter's point about records, and it is now a practical worry, because AI models are often trained on text written by other AI models.

Mathematics · AI

AI aimed at the proof checker, and it "proved" False

In the book: consistency is the least a system needs (section 8). And each of the Tortoise's records is made for one machine (section 2).

Now: Lean is a proof assistant, a program that checks every step of a proof by machine. Its users trust its small core, the part that does the checking. In the summer of 2026, a researcher at OpenAI set the company's internal AI models to hunt for inputs that would make this core accept a proof of False, a statement that is false by definition. A system that proves it can prove anything, because anything at all follows from a false starting point. The models found six routes. All needed deliberately built inputs, not ordinary proofs, and all six have been fixed, though later releases closed more possible routes of the same kind. For the faults in one part of the checker, Lean's developers say they were programming mistakes, not gaps in the theory behind it.

One more detail matches the dialogue. The fake proofs for five of the six faults were rejected by a separately written checker. The sixth fooled that checker too, and was caught only by a third, newer check. Since October 2026, Lean ships with several independently written checkers, and one command re-checks a proof with all of them.

Why it matters: this is the record-player game played against a formal system's own referee. And the defence follows the dialogue's logic: a record made for Record Player 1 usually does nothing to Record Player 2. Usually: one of the six broke both.

Physics

Which geometry is space? Still being measured

In the book: several geometries are consistent, so which one fits real space is a question for measurement, not proof (section 6).

Now: cosmologists measure it. Near heavy objects space is slightly curved (section 6); the question here is its overall shape. They sum up the answer in one number, called the curvature parameter: zero means flat, as on paper (Euclid's geometry); below zero means closed, like the sphere; above zero means open, like the saddle. On flat paper the three angles of a triangle add up to 180 degrees; on a sphere they add up to more, and on a saddle to less. So measuring a big enough triangle tells you which world you are in. The triangles cosmologists check span the visible universe.

The Planck satellite team's result (published 2020), joined with galaxy surveys, was 0.001, give or take 0.002, which fits a space that is flat on the largest scale. In 2025 the DESI project, a telescope survey that has mapped more than 14 million galaxies and quasars (very bright, very distant galaxy cores), reported no significant preference for curved space. One preprint (a paper posted before other scientists have reviewed it) from September 2026, using different measurements, finds what its authors call a mild lean toward the saddle, about twice its give-or-take away from flat. All of this covers only the part of the universe we can see.

Why it matters: the fifth postulate became something you measure with telescopes. Euclid's answer still fits the best data, but only as a measurement that a better one could overturn.
