Contracrostipunctus + Consistency, Completeness, and Geometry

GEB

A Tortoise keeps bringing his friend a record that shakes the friend's record player to pieces, however good the player. Hofstadter uses the story as a picture of Gödel's theorem, which says that no fixed set of rules for arithmetic can produce every true statement about numbers. This page covers the dialogue Contracrostipunctus and Chapter IV, which asks what it means for a system of rules to be consistent and complete, and finds the answer in a 2,000-year argument about parallel lines.

The Tortoise, the Crab, and a goblet with four letters on it

The story. Achilles visits the Tortoise, who tells him about a long contest with his friend the Crab. The Crab had bought a record player that the salesman called Perfect: it could reproduce any sound at all. The Tortoise came to visit with a record titled "I Cannot Be Played on Record Player 1". A few notes in, the player began to shake, and then it fell to pieces. The Tortoise had studied how the player was built and had put on the record exactly the sounds that would make it rattle itself apart.

The Crab bought a better player. The Tortoise came back with "I Cannot Be Played on Record Player 2". And so on. In the end the Crab orders a player that looks at each record first and rebuilds itself if the record seems dangerous. Does that one win? The Tortoise won't say. He only asks whether Achilles knows Gödel's theorem.

Then the roles turn around. As Achilles gets up to leave, he gives the Tortoise a present: a goblet, said to be the last piece made by a glassblower named Bach, with the letters B, A, C, H cut into it. The Tortoise admits that he has a search of his own, for a Perfect goblet. He notices that the four letters are also four notes, picks up his violin and plays them. On the last note the goblet shatters, and the dialogue stops dead. The goblet has met the Crab's fate: it was broken by a tune made for it, the one spelled on its own side. This time the Tortoise is the one left holding the pieces.

The form: Bach's last fugue

A fugue is a piece where several voices (separate melody lines) enter one after another with the same tune and weave together. The craft of fitting melodies against each other is called counterpoint. In his last years Bach wrote The Art of Fugue, a set of fugues and canons all built on one tune, and gave each fugue the Latin title Contrapunctus.

The last one is unfinished. Near the point where the manuscript breaks off, Bach brings in a new tune made of his own name. Musicians name notes by letters. In the German way of naming them, four notes that sit side by side on the piano are called B, A, C and H. So Bach's name is a four-note tune:

In the dialogue the Tortoise says this tune is the same played upside down (every step up becomes a step down) and backwards (last note first). Is he right? Press the second button.

The hidden trickThe title joins Contrapunctus to acrostic, a text whose first letters spell a message. Take the first letter of each speech in the dialogue, in order, and they spell a sentence. The sentence hides something in turn.
Hofstadter's Contracrostipunctus Acrostically Backwards Spells 'J. S. Bach'

So the dialogue signs itself the way Bach signed his fugue: with a name hidden in the material. Achilles even remarks, in passing, that Lewis Carroll liked to hide words in the first letters of his poems. And the dialogue ends as the fugue does, by breaking off in the middle. Shortly before, the two friends had been talking about how hard it is for an author to surprise anyone with an ending, since the reader can feel how few pages are left.

2

The record-player game

Play the Tortoise's game. A high-fidelity player (one that reproduces sound faithfully) plays ordinary records perfectly. A cheap low-fidelity player can only manage some of the notes. Try each record on each player.

Player
Record

A sketch. The tunes, and each player's "weak note", are made up for this demo; the dialogue doesn't give that detail.

Once each player has met the record made for it, every row of the table ends in "no", for two different reasons. The cheap player is safe from the Tortoise, but only because it can't make the sounds that would hurt it, and it can't make many other sounds either. Each high-fidelity player is good enough to start making the sounds that destroy it, so there is one sound it never finishes reproducing: the one on its own record. And if you buy Record Player 2 and give it the record that broke Record Player 1, it plays without trouble. No record is unplayable in general. Each one is aimed at a single machine.

The Tortoise's moral is that the fault isn't in any one player. It is in the hope for a Perfect one. Mathematicians once had the same hope: the Introduction told how, around 1900, they set out to build one perfect system of rules for all of mathematics.

The idea the dialogue sets upGödel's theorem ended that hope: in any formal system (starting statements plus mechanical rules for making new ones) that is strong enough to talk about whole numbers, and that never contradicts itself, there is a true statement the rules can never produce. The Tortoise's game is a picture of that theorem, with record players standing for formal systems. The chapter now asks what the picture shows: what it means for a formal system to be consistent (roughly: it never contradicts itself), what it means for it to be complete (roughly: it can produce every true statement), and what it is about a strong one that keeps it from being Perfect.
Chapter IV
Consistency, Completeness, and Geometry
3

One object, two meanings

A record's grooves have an obvious meaning: music. That is the meaning the record was made for, and Hofstadter calls it the explicit meaning. The Tortoise's records carry a second one. The same grooves, played on one particular machine, are a set of instructions for shaking that machine apart. Nobody would see this by listening. It is an implicit meaning, and it only appears when you line the sounds up against the design of the player.

Lining things up like this has come up before. An isomorphism is a match between two things whose parts correspond one-to-one, like a map and its city. Chapter II built a tiny formal system, the pq-system, whose theorems (the strings of symbols its rules can produce) are things like --p---q-----. The symbols mean nothing by themselves. But read p as "plus", q as "equals" and each group of hyphens as a number, and that theorem says "2 plus 3 equals 5". A way of reading symbols like this is called an interpretation. This one fits, because every theorem turns into a true sentence about addition.

The chapter's first point is that one object can have several interpretations at once, each through a different isomorphism. The dialogue itself is an example. On the surface it is a story. Its two halves match each other: record player and goblet, record and violin tune, Crab and Tortoise. Its first letters spell a message. And the whole thing lines up with Gödel's theorem:

Two things don't match up. The player falling to pieces has no partner in mathematics: a formal system doesn't break, it simply never produces the string. And one thing in mathematics has no partner in the story. Gödel's theorem is about systems that never contradict themselves, and nothing in the Tortoise's game stands for a contradiction. That is the next topic.

Gödel's string has two meanings in the same way the record has. Read one way it is a plain statement about numbers. Read another way it talks about the system it is written in. Later chapters build it; this one prepares the ground by asking how symbols get meanings at all, and what can go wrong.

4

Break a system, then mend it without touching it

1The system, and a reading that fits

Here is the whole pq-system. It has a pattern for its axioms (the strings you are given for free) and one rule.

Axioms

Any string of the shape xp-qx-, where x stands for any number of hyphens, the same number both times. For example --p-q---.

Rule

From a theorem, make a new one by adding one hyphen just before the q and one at the very end. From --p-q--- you get --p--q----.

Under the reading "plus" and "equals", the axioms say "x plus 1 equals x + 1" and the rule keeps both sides in step, so every theorem is a true addition.

2Add an axiom, and the reading breaks

Now Hofstadter adds a second pattern of axioms: any string of the shape xp-qx, one hyphen shorter at the end. That makes --p-q-- a theorem. Read the old way it says "2 plus 1 equals 2".

The grid below shows the damage. Each cell is one string: the row gives the two groups before the q, the column gives the number of hyphens after it. Turn the extra axioms on, then try the other reading of q.

With the extra axioms and the reading "equals", some theorems come out false. (When a system produces a string as a theorem, mathematicians say it proves the statement the string stands for. So this system proves false things.) Its theorems even disagree with each other: "2 plus 1 equals 2" and "2 plus 1 equals 3" can't both hold. A system that does this, under a given reading, is called inconsistent.

3Change the reading, and the clash is gone

But look at what the other reading did. Nothing about the system changed: same axioms, same rule, same theorems. Read q as "is at least" (so --p-q-- says "2 plus 1 is at least 2") and every theorem is true again. The contradiction was never in the strings. It was between the strings and the words we had attached to them.

Consistency needs an interpretationA formal system by itself is just strings; strings can't contradict each other. A system together with an interpretation is consistent when every theorem comes out as a true statement, and inconsistent when at least one comes out false. Change the interpretation and the verdict can change. (The Introduction's short version, "never contradicts itself", is the most visible case: if two theorems contradict each other, one of them must be false.)

Notice also that swapping the meaning of q is something no rule of the system could do. You did it from outside, the way Chapter I's puzzle was settled by stepping outside the rules.

5

The same thing happened to geometry

Around 300 BC the Greek mathematician Euclid wrote the Elements, which derives all of geometry from a few starting statements. He called them postulates; they are axioms under an older name. There are five. In plain words:

  1. A straight line can be drawn between any two points.
  2. A straight line can be extended as far as you like.
  3. A circle can be drawn around any point, with any size.
  4. All right angles (square corners) are equal.
  5. Through a point beside a line, exactly one line can be drawn that never meets the first. (This is a later, simpler wording. It is the parallel postulate.)

The first four are short and feel obvious. The fifth is different. Euclid's own wording of it runs to several lines, and it makes a claim about what happens infinitely far away. Euclid himself put off using it for as long as he could. For two thousand years, mathematicians tried to show it wasn't needed as an axiom, by proving it from the other four.

In 1733 an Italian priest, Girolamo Saccheri, tried a new route. He assumed the fifth postulate was false and worked out what followed, expecting to hit a contradiction. That would prove the postulate had to be true. He derived one strange result after another. No contradiction came. Finally he reached a result so odd (two straight lines that draw closer and closer for ever without meeting) that he declared it against the very nature of a straight line, stopped, and published his book as a defence of Euclid.

He had found a new geometry and thrown it away. About a century later, in the 1820s and 1830s, János Bolyai in Hungary and Nikolai Lobachevsky in Russia each did the same work, independently, and drew the other conclusion: the strange results fit together and never clash, so they describe a geometry of their own. Geometries where the fifth postulate fails are called non-Euclidean.

What could such a geometry look like? Try three worlds. In each, L is a line and P is a point beside it. Turn a line through P and watch whether it meets L.

World

On the sphere, the second postulate holds only if "extended as far as you like" is read as "never comes to an end". A line that goes round and returns never ends, but it isn't infinitely long.

On flat paper the answer is Euclid's: one line misses. On the sphere, none does. On the saddle-shaped surface, a whole fan of lines misses. The first four postulates, as they stand, don't decide between flat paper and the saddle. The sphere is a harder case. It fits only once some words are read in a new way, and that is the next section.

6

What does "line" mean?

You may have objected to the last demo: those curves on the sphere aren't straight. That objection is exactly what stopped Saccheri. The word "line" came to him with a picture attached, and when his results stopped fitting the picture he trusted the picture.

But look at what Euclid's postulates actually pin down. They never say what a point or a line is. They only say how points and lines behave toward each other: two points have a line through them, lines can be extended, and so on. "Point" and "line" are undefined terms. Like p and q, they are symbols, and anything that behaves as the postulates demand is entitled to the names. The pq-system carries the same warning: it is easy to read "plus" into p and then believe things about p that the rules never said.

On a sphere, the shortest path between two places runs along a great circle, a circle as big as the sphere allows, like the equator. Planes fly along them. So read "line" as "great circle". One problem remains, and the drawing shows it.

So the mending takes two re-readings: "line" means great circle, and "point" means a pair of opposite spots. With those, two points fix one line again, and every two lines meet in exactly one point, so there are no parallels at all. It is the move from section 4. There, a new axiom clashed with the old reading of q, and a new reading removed the clash. Here, a new fifth postulate ("through a point beside a line, no line can be drawn that never meets the first") clashes with the old reading of "line", and a new reading removes it.

One more piece of the picture. Drop the fifth postulate and keep the other four, and you have a smaller geometry, called absolute geometry. Everything it proves holds on flat paper and on the saddle alike, since both obey those four as they stand. (The sphere is left out, because it needed the words re-read.) Absolute geometry is the shared core. It can't settle the fifth postulate either way, so geometry forks: add the fifth and you get Euclid's geometry, add a rival and you get another.

What geometry learnedThere isn't one true geometry that mathematics can prove. There are several, each consistent, and the fifth postulate is a choice between them. Which one describes the space we live in is a question for measurement, not proof. It became a question for physics, and physics has since found that space is very slightly curved near heavy objects such as the Sun.

Kinds of consistency

Section 4 called a system consistent when every theorem "comes out true". True of what? Hofstadter splits that definition in two.

External consistency: the theorems agree with the world. Internal consistency: the theorems agree with each other. The second is the one that matters in mathematics, and it has a neat form: a system (with its interpretation) is internally consistent if you can imagine a world in which all its theorems are true together. Non-Euclidean geometry clashed with everyone's picture of the real world, yet it was internally consistent, because there are worlds (a sphere, a saddle) where it holds.

Hofstadter's example has just three theorems: TbZ, ZbE and EbT. Let T, Z and E be the Tortoise, Zeno (an ancient Greek philosopher who turns up in the book's dialogues) and a third character, Egbert. Choose a reading for b.

b means

Under the second reading, each theorem on its own could be true. Only the three together are impossible. Inconsistency can hide in the combination.

"Imaginable" comes in degrees, so consistency does too. Hofstadter sorts imagined worlds by which rules they are allowed to break. (The examples are this page's own.)

  • Logically consistent: no statement is both true and false. Almost any story passes.
  • Mathematically consistent: it also keeps the facts of mathematics. A world where 2 plus 1 equals 2 fails here.
  • Physically consistent: it also keeps the laws of physics. A story with a machine that runs for ever without fuel fails here.
  • Biologically consistent: it also keeps the facts of biology. A story with a talking tortoise fails here.

That raises a question. Logic must be the same in every world we can talk about sensibly. Geometry, we have just seen, need not be. What about number theory: could there be worlds with different arithmetic? The chapter's answer is yes, in the way geometry allowed it. This is why geometry is in a chapter about Gödel. Number theory has a shared core, like absolute geometry, that holds in every such world. But Gödel's theorem will show that the core leaves some statements undecided, as the four postulates leave the fifth. Add such a statement, or add its opposite, and you get rival number theories. The book works this out in Chapter XIV.

Escher's Relativity is a picture of a clash that hides in the combination, like the three theorems above. In this 1953 lithograph (a print made from a drawing on stone), people climb staircases in one building, but "down" points in three different directions. Each staircase with its walker makes sense. The building as a whole could not exist. See the print at mcescher.com.

Hofstadter's point is about us, the viewers. We recognise a staircase instantly, and once we have, we can't un-see it. So when the whole picture clashes, we don't escape by deciding the staircases are something else. We keep the small, familiar readings and accept an impossible world. It is Saccheri's problem in a picture: a familiar meaning that won't let go, even when letting go would dissolve the clash.

8

Completeness, and the limit on strong systems

1The definition

Consistency, as section 4 defined it, asks: is every theorem true? Turn the question around and you get the other property. A system with an interpretation is complete when every true statement that can be written in the system's symbols is a theorem.

Hofstadter puts the pair like this. Consistency is the least you need before symbols can be said to mean anything: whatever the system produces is true. Completeness is the most you could hope for: whatever is true, the system produces.

The limit "that can be written in the system's symbols" matters. The original pq-system is complete, yet it knows nothing about multiplication or even "2 plus 3 plus 4". Its strings can only say that two numbers add up to a third, and it produces every true string of that kind. That is all completeness asks.

2A consistent system with a gap

Here is the grid again, now with both verdicts, and with the extra axioms on and q read as "is at least". A dashed cell is a true statement that is not a theorem.

Reading q as "is at least" made the system consistent, but it also made lots of new statements true, such as "2 plus 2 is at least 1", and the system proves only a few of them. The dashed cells are truths the system can't reach. There are two ways to close such a gap:

  • Add rules, so the system proves more.
  • Tighten the interpretation, so fewer statements count as true. The third reading in the grid, "equals, or is 1 more than", does this, and the gap closes.
3Number theory can't close its gap

For this toy system, tightening is the sensible choice. For number theory it isn't on offer. Its symbols are meant to say plus, times and equals; read them as anything looser and you are no longer doing number theory. That leaves adding rules. And here is the Tortoise again. Gödel showed that whenever a consistent system is strong enough to express number theory (addition and multiplication of whole numbers), there is a true statement it can't prove. His method, as the Introduction sketched, was to code statements as numbers, so that a system about numbers can make statements about itself, including one that says "I am not a theorem". The proof itself comes in later chapters. Add that statement as a new axiom and you have a new, stronger system, which has a new unprovable truth of its own. Each new record player gets its own record.

Back to the record playersA weak system is the cheap player. It can be complete about the little it can say, as the pq-system is, but most truths about numbers can't even be written in it. A strong system is the high-fidelity player. It can state every truth about numbers, and that same power lets it describe itself, which is what allows a statement to be aimed at it. Neither is a Perfect system for number theory, and no consistent system can be.
9

Recap: the dialogue and chapter in one breath

Symbols mean only what a matching with the world lets them mean. Choose the matching and you decide whether a system is consistent, and whether it is complete. For a system strong enough to express the addition and multiplication of whole numbers, and read as arithmetic, you can't have both.

Dialogue

A record for every player

Each good player is broken by a record aimed at it; the cheap one is safe only because it can do so little. The goblet replays the trick on the Tortoise, and the first letters hide a name, as Bach's last fugue does.

Formal systems

Consistent and complete

Consistent: every theorem is true under the interpretation. Complete: every truth the symbols can state is a theorem. Both depend on how the symbols are read.

Geometry

"Line" is an undefined term

Deny Euclid's fifth postulate and no contradiction follows, only new geometries. A clash between axioms and meanings can be cured by changing the meanings.
